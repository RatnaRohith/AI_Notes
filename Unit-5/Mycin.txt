Typical Expert System: MYCIN
5
1. What is MYCIN?

MYCIN was one of the earliest and most influential medical expert systems developed at Stanford University in the 1970s.

It was designed to help doctors diagnose bacterial infections and recommend appropriate antibiotic treatments.

MYCIN = Medical diagnosis + treatment recommendation using rule-based reasoning

It is a classic example of a rule-based expert system.

2. Purpose of MYCIN

MYCIN was primarily developed to:

Identify the type of bacterial infection.
Determine the likely causative microorganism.
Recommend suitable antibiotics.
Suggest appropriate dosages.
Handle uncertain or incomplete information.

For example:

Patient information:

Patient has fever.
Blood culture is positive.
Gram stain indicates gram-negative bacteria.
Bacteria have a particular morphology.

MYCIN could reason from these facts and suggest possible organisms and treatment.

Main components
1. Knowledge Base

Contains medical knowledge represented mainly as IF–THEN rules.

Example:

IF
    the infection is bacterial
AND
    the organism is gram-negative
THEN
    consider an appropriate antibiotic

MYCIN contained a large collection of such rules.

2. Working Memory

Stores information about the current patient.

For example:

Patient has fever.
Patient has blood infection.
Gram stain = gram-negative.
Organism morphology = rod.

The working memory changes as MYCIN asks questions and derives new facts.

3. Inference Engine

The inference engine applies rules to the available patient information.

It determines:

Known facts
     ↓
Find applicable rules
     ↓
Apply rules
     ↓
Generate new facts
     ↓
Reach diagnosis
     ↓
Recommend treatment

MYCIN used backward chaining extensively.

4. Certainty Factors

One of MYCIN's most important contributions was its use of certainty factors (CFs) to represent uncertainty.

Medical information is rarely completely certain.

For example:

Organism X causes infection
Certainty Factor = 0.8

This means MYCIN has relatively strong confidence in that conclusion.

A simplified interpretation is:

CF	Interpretation
+1.0	Completely certain
+0.8	Strongly supported
+0.5	Moderately supported
0	Unknown
Negative	Evidence against the hypothesis

Thus, MYCIN could reason even when information was incomplete or uncertain.

4. Rule-Based Reasoning in MYCIN

A MYCIN rule could be expressed conceptually as:

IF
    the patient has meningitis
AND
    the patient has a bacterial infection
THEN
    recommend antibiotic therapy

The inference engine checks whether the conditions are satisfied.

Example

Suppose:

Fact 1: Patient has fever.
Fact 2: Patient has meningitis.
Fact 3: Infection is probably bacterial.

The system may apply:

IF meningitis
AND bacterial infection
THEN recommend antibiotic treatment

and generate:

Antibiotic treatment is recommended.
5. Backward Chaining in MYCIN

MYCIN generally starts with a goal or hypothesis and works backward to determine whether the available evidence supports it.

For example:

Goal:
Is the patient infected with Organism X?
          │
          ▼
What conditions support Organism X?
          │
          ▼
Check patient facts
          │
          ▼
Ask doctor for missing information
          │
          ▼
Calculate confidence
          │
          ▼
Accept / reject hypothesis

This is called backward chaining.

Why backward chaining?

MYCIN did not need to apply every medical rule.

Instead, it focused on rules relevant to the current diagnostic problem.

6. Explanation Facility

A major feature of MYCIN was its ability to explain its reasoning.

A doctor could ask questions such as:

WHY?

or

HOW?

The system could explain:

Why it asked a particular question.
Why it reached a particular diagnosis.
Which rules were used.
What evidence supported the conclusion.

This is important because doctors need to understand and evaluate an expert system's recommendation rather than simply receive an unexplained answer.

7. Knowledge Acquisition

MYCIN also demonstrated an important issue in expert systems:

How can expert knowledge be collected and represented in a computer?

Medical experts provided knowledge that was converted into rules.

For example:

Medical Expert
      ↓
Medical knowledge
      ↓
Knowledge Engineer
      ↓
IF–THEN rules
      ↓
MYCIN Knowledge Base

This process is called knowledge acquisition.

8. MYCIN's Interaction with the Doctor

MYCIN could ask questions such as:

Is the patient experiencing fever?

Is the blood culture positive?

What is the Gram stain result?

What is the morphology of the organism?

The doctor provides answers, and MYCIN uses them as facts.

Therefore:

Doctor
  ↓
Patient information
  ↓
MYCIN
  ↓
Reasoning
  ↓
Diagnosis
  ↓
Treatment recommendation
9. Advantages of MYCIN
1. Expert-level reasoning

MYCIN demonstrated that a computer could perform complex medical reasoning using a knowledge base.

2. Handles uncertainty

Certainty factors allowed the system to work with uncertain medical evidence.

3. Explanation capability

It could explain why it reached a conclusion.

4. Knowledge separated from reasoning

The medical knowledge was stored separately from the inference mechanism.

This made the architecture reusable.

5. Interactive

The system could ask questions and obtain additional information from the user.

10. Limitations of MYCIN
1. Limited domain

MYCIN was designed for a relatively specific medical problem.

It was not a general-purpose medical doctor.

2. Knowledge acquisition bottleneck

Converting expert medical knowledge into rules was difficult and time-consuming.

3. Dependence on rules

Its performance depended heavily on the quality and completeness of its knowledge base.

4. Not a replacement for doctors

MYCIN was designed as a decision-support system, not as an autonomous physician.

5. Practical deployment issues

Despite impressive experimental results, MYCIN was not widely deployed for routine clinical practice.

11. Importance of MYCIN in AI

MYCIN is historically important because it demonstrated several fundamental ideas in Expert Systems:
