Logic concepts:  Resolution lecture
Resolution in Artificial Intelligence — Lecture
1. Introduction

Resolution is a fundamental inference rule used in Artificial Intelligence and mathematical logic to derive new conclusions from existing knowledge.

It is especially important in:

Propositional Logic
First-Order Predicate Logic
Automated Theorem Proving
Logic Programming
Knowledge Representation

The main idea is:

Resolution proves a statement by showing that assuming its negation leads to a contradiction.

Resolution is particularly useful because it provides a single, systematic inference rule that can be implemented by computers.

2. Basic Idea of Resolution

Consider two clauses:

$$ P \lor Q $$

and

$$ \neg Q \lor R $$

Both clauses contain complementary literals:

$$ Q \quad \text{and} \quad \neg Q $$

Resolution eliminates these complementary literals and produces:

$$ P \lor R $$

This is called the resolvent.

Resolution Rule
$$ \frac{P \lor Q,\quad \neg Q \lor R}{P \lor R} $$

The general form is:

$$ \frac{A \lor B,\quad \neg B \lor C}{A \lor C} $$
3. What is a Clause?

A clause is a disjunction (OR) of literals.

For example:

$$ P \lor Q \lor R $$

is a clause.

A literal can be:

A positive proposition: \(P\)
A negated proposition: \(\neg P\)

Examples:

$$ P $$ $$ \neg Q $$ $$ P \lor \neg Q \lor R $$

are all clauses.

4. Conjunction of Clauses

A knowledge base normally consists of several clauses connected using AND.

For example:

$$ (P \lor Q) \land (\neg Q \lor R) \land (\neg R) $$

This is called Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF).

Resolution works directly on clauses in CNF.

5. Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF)

Before applying resolution, the logical expression is generally converted into CNF.

CNF has the form:

$$ C_1 \land C_2 \land C_3 \land \cdots $$

where each \(C_i\) is a disjunction of literals.

Example

Consider:

$$ P \rightarrow Q $$

Remove implication:

$$ \neg P \lor Q $$

This is already in CNF.

Another example:

$$ P \land (Q \lor R) $$

is already in CNF:

$$ P \land (Q \lor R) $$
6. Resolution in Propositional Logic

Suppose we have:

$$ P \lor Q $$

and

$$ \neg Q \lor R $$

Using resolution:

Step 1: Identify complementary literals
$$ Q,\quad \neg Q $$
Step 2: Remove them

Remaining literals:

$$ P,\quad R $$
Step 3: Combine them using OR
$$ P \lor R $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{P \lor R} $$

is the resolvent.

7. Resolution Refutation

Resolution is commonly used for proof by contradiction, called resolution refutation.

Suppose we want to prove:

$$ KB \models Q $$

where \(KB\) is the knowledge base.

Instead of directly proving \(Q\), we:

Take the knowledge base.
Negate the statement to be proved.
Add the negated statement to the knowledge base.
Convert everything into CNF.
Apply resolution repeatedly.
Try to derive the empty clause.

The empty clause is represented as:

$$ \boxed{\Box} $$

or sometimes:

$$ \boxed{\emptyset} $$

The empty clause represents a contradiction.

8. Resolution Refutation Procedure
Algorithm

Given:

$$ KB \models Q $$

Perform:

Step 1 — Negate the goal
$$ \neg Q $$
Step 2 — Add it to the knowledge base
$$ KB \land \neg Q $$
Step 3 — Convert to CNF

Convert all expressions into clauses.

Step 4 — Apply resolution

Find pairs of clauses containing complementary literals.

Step 5 — Generate resolvents

Continue applying resolution.

Step 6 — Check for contradiction

If we derive:

$$ \Box $$

then the original statement \(Q\) is proven.

9. Simple Example

Consider the knowledge base:

If it rains, the ground is wet.
$$ Rain \rightarrow Wet $$
It rains.
$$ Rain $$

Prove:

$$ Wet $$
Step 1: Convert the knowledge base into CNF

The first statement:

$$ Rain \rightarrow Wet $$

Remove implication:

$$ \neg Rain \lor Wet $$

The second statement:

$$ Rain $$

Therefore our clauses are:

$$ C_1 = \neg Rain \lor Wet $$ $$ C_2 = Rain $$
Step 2: Negate the goal

Goal:

$$ Wet $$

Negated goal:

$$ \neg Wet $$

Add it:

$$ C_3 = \neg Wet $$
Step 3: Apply resolution

Resolve:

$$ \neg Rain \lor Wet $$

with

$$ \neg Wet $$

The complementary literals are:

$$ Wet,\quad \neg Wet $$

Resolvent:

$$ \neg Rain $$

So:

$$ C_4 = \neg Rain $$

Now resolve:

$$ Rain $$

with

$$ \neg Rain $$

We obtain:

$$ \boxed{\Box} $$
10. Resolution Proof

The complete proof can be represented as:

$$ \begin{aligned} C_1 &: \neg Rain \lor Wet\\ C_2 &: Rain\\ C_3 &: \neg Wet\\ \hline C_4 &: \neg Rain\\ C_2 &: Rain\\ C_4 &: \neg Rain\\ \hline &\boxed{\Box} \end{aligned} $$

Since the empty clause is obtained:

$$ KB \models Wet $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{Wet\text{ is proved}} $$
11. Another Example

Given:

$$ P \lor Q $$ $$ \neg P \lor R $$ $$ \neg Q $$

Prove:

$$ R $$
Step 1: Negate the goal
$$ \neg R $$

Clauses:

$$ C_1=P\lor Q $$ $$ C_2=\neg P\lor R $$ $$ C_3=\neg Q $$ $$ C_4=\neg R $$
Step 2: Resolve \(C_1\) and \(C_3\)
$$ P\lor Q $$ $$ \neg Q $$

Resolvent:

$$ P $$
Step 3: Resolve \(P\) with \(C_2\)
$$ P $$ $$ \neg P\lor R $$

Resolvent:

$$ R $$
Step 4: Resolve \(R\) with \(\neg R\)
$$ R $$ $$ \neg R $$

Result:

$$ \boxed{\Box} $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{R} $$

is proved.

12. Resolution in First-Order Logic

Resolution can also be applied to First-Order Logic (FOL).

However, FOL introduces variables and predicates.

For example:

$$ Human(Socrates) $$ $$ Human(x) \rightarrow Mortal(x) $$

Goal:

$$ Mortal(Socrates) $$

The implication becomes:

$$ \neg Human(x) \lor Mortal(x) $$

We also have:

$$ Human(Socrates) $$

Using unification, we substitute:

$$ x = Socrates $$

Then:

$$ \neg Human(Socrates) \lor Mortal(Socrates) $$

and

$$ Human(Socrates) $$

resolve to:

$$ Mortal(Socrates) $$

This shows the relationship between:

Unification + Resolution = powerful inference mechanism for First-Order Logic.

13. Resolution and Unification

In propositional logic, complementary literals must match directly.

For example:

$$ P $$

and

$$ \neg P $$

In First-Order Logic, predicates may contain variables.

Example:

$$ Likes(x, IceCream) $$

and

$$ \neg Likes(John, IceCream) $$

These can be unified using:

$$ x = John $$

Then:

$$ Likes(John, IceCream) $$

and

$$ \neg Likes(John, IceCream) $$

are complementary.

Therefore, they can be resolved.

14. Resolution Rule in First-Order Logic

Consider:

$$ P(x) \lor Q(x) $$

and:

$$ \neg Q(John) \lor R(John) $$

Unify:

$$ x = John $$

First clause becomes:

$$ P(John) \lor Q(John) $$

Now resolve \(Q(John)\) and \(\neg Q(John)\):

$$ P(John) \lor R(John) $$

This is the resolvent.

15. Important Terminology
Term	Meaning
Literal	Proposition or its negation
Clause	OR of literals
CNF	AND of clauses
Resolvent	New clause generated by resolution
Complementary literals	\(P\) and \(\neg P\)
Resolution	Inference rule that eliminates complementary literals
Empty clause	Represents contradiction
Resolution refutation	Proving a statement by deriving contradiction from its negation
Unification	Finding substitutions that make predicates identical
16. Resolution vs Modus Ponens

These are different inference mechanisms.

Modus Ponens

Given:

$$ P \rightarrow Q $$

and:

$$ P $$

we conclude:

$$ Q $$
Resolution

Convert:

$$ P \rightarrow Q $$

to:

$$ \neg P \lor Q $$

Then resolve:

$$ \neg P \lor Q $$

with:

$$ P $$

to obtain:

$$ Q $$

Thus resolution can simulate many logical inference steps after conversion to CNF.

17. Advantages of Resolution
1. Simple inference rule

Resolution uses a single basic inference mechanism.

2. Suitable for automation

It can be implemented by computers for automated theorem proving.

3. Works with propositional logic

It provides a systematic proof mechanism.

4. Works with First-Order Logic

With unification, resolution becomes a powerful FOL inference technique.

5. Refutation is systematic

The proof continues until either:

Empty clause is found → proved
No new useful clauses can be generated → cannot prove the statement using the current search
18. Limitations

Resolution can suffer from combinatorial explosion because many possible pairs of clauses may need to be considered.

Other issues include:

Large number of generated clauses
High computational cost
Need to convert expressions into CNF
Search-space explosion
First-order resolution requires unification

Therefore, practical systems use strategies such as:

Set of Support
Unit Preference
Subsumption
Ordered Resolution
Input Resolution
19. Key Exam Point

Remember this sequence:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Knowledge Base} \rightarrow \text{Negate Goal} \rightarrow \text{CNF} \rightarrow \text{Clauses} \rightarrow \text{Resolution} \rightarrow \Box } $$

If the empty clause is obtained:

$$ \boxed{\Box} $$

then a contradiction has been found, and the original goal is proved.

20. Short Definition for Examination

Resolution is an inference rule used in propositional and first-order logic that derives a new clause by eliminating complementary literals from two clauses. In resolution refutation, the negation of the goal is added to the knowledge base, and resolution is repeatedly applied until the empty clause is derived, proving the original goal.

One-line example
$$ (P\lor Q),\quad(\neg Q\lor R) $$ $$ \therefore \boxed{P\lor R} $$

This is the resolution principle.
