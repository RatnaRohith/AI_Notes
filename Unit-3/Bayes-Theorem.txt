Bayes Probabilistic Inference — Lecture
1. Introduction

In Artificial Intelligence, an agent often has to make decisions when it does not have complete or certain information.

For example:

A patient has symptoms. Does the patient have a disease?
A sensor gives a reading. Is the machine actually faulty?
An email contains certain words. Is it spam?
A robot observes an object. Is it an obstacle?

In such situations, probabilistic inference allows an AI system to reason about uncertainty.

Bayesian inference is the process of updating the probability of a hypothesis when new evidence becomes available.

2. What is Probabilistic Inference?

Probabilistic inference is the process of calculating the probability of an unknown event or hypothesis based on available information and evidence.

Suppose:

\(D\) = patient has a disease
\(T\) = patient has a positive test result

We may want to calculate:

$$ P(D|T) $$

This means:

Probability that the patient has the disease given that the test is positive.

The important point is that AI does not simply ask:

"Is the patient sick?"

Instead, it asks:

"How likely is it that the patient is sick, given the evidence?"

Bayes' theorem provides a mathematical method for updating beliefs when new evidence is observed.

4. Understanding the Four Terms
4.1 Prior Probability — \(P(A)\)

The prior is our initial belief about an event before seeing new evidence.

Example:

If 1% of people have a particular disease:

$$ P(D)=0.01 $$

This is the prior probability.

4.2 Likelihood — \(P(B|A)\)

Likelihood tells us how probable the evidence is when the hypothesis is true.

Example:

If a person has the disease, there is a 90% chance that the medical test is positive:

$$ P(T|D)=0.90 $$
4.3 Evidence — \(P(B)\)

Evidence is the overall probability of observing the evidence.

For example:

$$ P(T) $$

means the probability that a randomly selected person gets a positive test.

4.4 Posterior Probability — \(P(A|B)\)

The posterior is the updated probability after observing the evidence.

For example:

$$ P(D|T) $$

means:

Probability that the patient has the disease given that the test is positive.

This is usually the quantity we want to calculate.

5. Basic Bayesian Inference Process

Bayesian reasoning can be viewed as:

Prior belief → Observe evidence → Update belief → Posterior belief

For example:

Initial belief
     ↓
Patient has 1% chance of disease
     ↓
Medical test is positive
     ↓
Apply Bayes' theorem
     ↓
Updated probability
     ↓
Posterior probability of disease
6. Worked Example — Medical Diagnosis

Suppose:

Probability of disease = 1%
Probability of positive test when disease exists = 90%
Probability of positive test when disease does not exist = 5%

Given that the test is positive, find the probability that the person actually has the disease.

Given
$$ P(D)=0.01 $$ $$ P(T|D)=0.90 $$ $$ P(T|\neg D)=0.05 $$

We need:

$$ P(D|T) $$
Step 1: Calculate \(P(T)\)

Using the law of total probability:

$$ P(T)=P(T|D)P(D)+P(T|\neg D)P(\neg D) $$

Since:

$$ P(\neg D)=1-P(D)=0.99 $$

Therefore:

$$ P(T)=(0.90)(0.01)+(0.05)(0.99) $$ $$ P(T)=0.009+0.0495 $$ $$ P(T)=0.0585 $$
Step 2: Apply Bayes' theorem
$$ P(D|T)= \frac{P(T|D)P(D)}{P(T)} $$ $$ P(D|T)= \frac{(0.90)(0.01)}{0.0585} $$ $$ P(D|T)\approx0.1538 $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{P(D|T)\approx15.38\%} $$
Important observation

Although the test is positive, the probability of actually having the disease is only about 15.38%.

This demonstrates why Bayesian reasoning is important.

A positive test does not automatically mean that the hypothesis is true.

7. Why Does This Happen?

The reason is the relatively low prior probability.

Only 1% of people have the disease.

Among 10,000 people:

100 have the disease.
9,900 do not.

Assume the test characteristics above.

Among 100 diseased people

90 test positive.

Among 9,900 healthy people

5% test positive:

$$ 9900\times0.05=495 $$

Therefore, positive results:

$$ 90+495=585 $$

Actual diseased people among positive results:

$$ 90 $$

Therefore:

$$ P(D|T)=\frac{90}{585}\approx15.38\% $$

This is an excellent intuitive explanation of Bayesian inference.

9. Bayesian Network

A Bayesian Network is a graphical representation of probabilistic relationships between variables.

It consists of:

Nodes → random variables
Directed edges → dependencies
Conditional probability tables (CPTs) → probabilities

Example:

     Rain
      ↓
    Wet Road
      ↓
   Accident

Here:

Rain influences whether the road is wet.
Wet road influences the probability of an accident.

A more complex example:

          Rain
         /    \
        ↓      ↓
   Wet Road   Traffic
        \      /
         ↓    ↓
         Accident

The network allows an AI system to reason about uncertain events.

10. Conditional Probability

Bayesian inference is based heavily on conditional probability.

Conditional probability is:

$$ P(A|B) $$

Read as:

Probability of A given B.

For example:

$$ P(Rain|Cloudy) $$

means:

Probability of rain given that the sky is cloudy.

It is calculated as:

$$ P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} $$
11. Bayes' Theorem Derivation

Starting with conditional probability:

$$ P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} $$

Similarly:

$$ P(B|A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)} $$

Therefore:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(B|A)P(A) $$

Substituting:

$$ P(A|B)= \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)} $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}} $$

14. Bayesian Updating

One of the most important concepts is updating beliefs.

Suppose an AI initially believes:

$$ P(A)=0.30 $$

New evidence \(B\) is observed.

The AI calculates:

$$ P(A|B) $$

and obtains:

$$ P(A|B)=0.75 $$

The AI has therefore updated its belief:

Before evidence: 30%
          ↓
     New evidence
          ↓
After evidence: 75%

This is the fundamental idea behind Bayesian reasoning.

15. Multiple Pieces of Evidence

Bayesian inference can also incorporate multiple observations.

Suppose an AI receives:

$$ E_1,E_2,E_3 $$

It can calculate:

$$ P(H|E_1,E_2,E_3) $$

where \(H\) is a hypothesis.

For example:

Symptoms
   +

   +

   ↓

   ↓
Probability of disease

As additional evidence becomes available, the probability can be updated.

16. Naïve Bayes Classifier

A very important AI application of Bayes' theorem is the Naïve Bayes classifier.

It is a probabilistic classification algorithm based on Bayes' theorem.

For a class \(C\) and features \(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n\):

$$ P(C|X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n) $$

Using the naïve conditional-independence assumption:

$$ P(C|X_1,\ldots,X_n) \propto P(C)\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(X_i|C) $$

The classifier chooses the class with the highest probability.

17. Example — Spam Classification

Suppose an email contains:

"Win free money"

Features:

\(X_1=\) "win"
\(X_2=\) "free"
\(X_3=\) "money"

Possible classes:

Spam
Not Spam

The classifier calculates approximately:

$$ P(Spam|Win,Free,Money) $$

and:

$$ P(NotSpam|Win,Free,Money) $$

If:

$$ P(Spam|features)>P(NotSpam|features) $$

then:

$$ \boxed{\text{Classify as Spam}} $$
18. Bayesian Reasoning Example in Robotics

Consider a robot searching for a charging station.

Initially:

Location A: 40%
Location B: 35%
Location C: 25%

The robot receives a sensor reading indicating that it detects a strong charging signal near Location C.

It updates its beliefs:

Before sensor:
A = 40%
B = 35%
C = 25%

       ↓ Sensor evidence

After sensor:
A = 15%
B = 20%
C = 65%

The robot can now make a better decision based on the updated probabilities.

19. Advantages of Bayesian Inference
1. Handles uncertainty

It allows AI systems to reason when information is incomplete.

2. Incorporates prior knowledge

Previous knowledge can be represented using prior probabilities.

3. Updates beliefs

New evidence can change the probability of hypotheses.

4. Supports decision making

An AI agent can select an action based on the most probable situation.

5. Works with noisy information

Real-world sensors and observations are rarely perfect.

20. Limitations
1. Requires probability information

Prior and conditional probabilities may be difficult to obtain.

2. Computational complexity

Large Bayesian models can require significant computation.

3. Dependence assumptions

Some simplified models, such as Naïve Bayes, assume conditional independence that may not be completely true.

4. Poor probabilities can produce poor results

If the prior or likelihood estimates are inaccurate, the resulting inference can also be inaccurate.

23. Exam-Oriented Summary

Bayesian probabilistic inference is a method of reasoning under uncertainty in which an AI system uses prior knowledge and newly observed evidence to calculate an updated probability.

The central formula is:

$$ \boxed{P(H|E)=\frac{P(E|H)P(H)}{P(E)}} $$

where:

\(H\) = hypothesis
\(E\) = evidence
\(P(H)\) = prior
\(P(E|H)\) = likelihood
\(P(E)\) = evidence
\(P(H|E)\) = posterior

The general process is:

        Prior Knowledge
              ↓
          Hypothesis
              ↓
        Observe Evidence
              ↓
       Apply Bayes' Theorem
              ↓
       Calculate Posterior
              ↓
       Updated Belief
              ↓
       AI Decision

Core idea:

Bayesian inference allows an AI system to start with a belief, observe evidence, and mathematically update that belief.
