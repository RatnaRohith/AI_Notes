Dempster–Shafer Theory — Lecture
1. Introduction

Dempster–Shafer Theory (DST) is a mathematical framework for reasoning under uncertainty.

It was developed by Arthur Dempster and later extended/formalized by Glenn Shafer.

DST is useful when an AI system has incomplete, uncertain, or imprecise information.

Example

Suppose an AI system is diagnosing whether a patient has:

Flu
COVID
Some other disease

A medical test may indicate:

“The patient has either Flu or COVID, but the test cannot distinguish between them.”

Traditional probability often requires distributing probability between individual possibilities.

DST allows us to assign belief directly to the set {Flu, COVID}.

This is one of the major differences between probability theory and Dempster–Shafer theory.

2. Why Do We Need Dempster–Shafer Theory?

In real-world AI systems, information is often:

Incomplete
Uncertain
Imprecise
Conflicting
Obtained from multiple sources

For example:

Sensor 1 says the object is probably a car.

Sensor 2 says the object is either a car or truck, but cannot distinguish them.

DST provides a way to represent such uncertainty without forcing us to make an arbitrary decision.

3. Basic Idea

Suppose the possible hypotheses are:

$$ \Theta = \{A,B\} $$

where:

\(A\) = Car
\(B\) = Truck

In ordinary probability, we might have:

$$ P(A)=0.6 $$ $$ P(B)=0.4 $$

But DST can represent:

$$ m(\{A\})=0.4 $$ $$ m(\{B\})=0.2 $$ $$ m(\{A,B\})=0.4 $$

The last value means:

We have 40% of our evidence supporting the proposition that the answer is either A or B, but we don't know which one.

This is called uncommitted belief or ignorance.

4. Frame of Discernment

The first concept in DST is the Frame of Discernment.

It represents the complete set of possible hypotheses.

It is denoted by:

$$ \Theta $$
Example

For weather prediction:

$$ \Theta=\{Rain, Sunny, Cloudy\} $$

For vehicle classification:

$$ \Theta=\{Car, Truck, Bus\} $$

For medical diagnosis:

$$ \Theta=\{Flu,COVID,Malaria\} $$

The elements of \(\Theta\) are called hypotheses.

5. Power Set

DST considers not only individual hypotheses but also sets of hypotheses.

The set of all possible subsets of \(\Theta\) is called the Power Set.

It is denoted by:

$$ 2^\Theta $$
Example

If:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B\} $$

then:

$$ 2^\Theta= \{\emptyset,\{A\},\{B\},\{A,B\}\} $$

For three hypotheses:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B,C\} $$

the power set contains:

$$ 2^3=8 $$

subsets:

$$ \emptyset $$ $$ \{A\} $$ $$ \{B\} $$ $$ \{C\} $$ $$ \{A,B\} $$ $$ \{A,C\} $$ $$ \{B,C\} $$ $$ \{A,B,C\} $$
6. Basic Probability Assignment

The central concept in DST is the Basic Probability Assignment, commonly called BPA or Basic Belief Assignment.

It is represented by:

$$ m(A) $$

where \(A\) can be a subset of \(\Theta\).

The BPA represents how much evidence directly supports proposition \(A\).

The following conditions must hold:

Condition 1
$$ m(\emptyset)=0 $$
Condition 2
$$ \sum_{A\subseteq\Theta}m(A)=1 $$

Therefore, all the available evidence must be distributed among the subsets.

7. Example of BPA

Consider:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B,C\} $$

Suppose evidence gives:

$$ m(\{A\})=0.5 $$ $$ m(\{B\})=0.2 $$ $$ m(\{A,B\})=0.2 $$ $$ m(\{A,B,C\})=0.1 $$

Check:

$$ 0.5+0.2+0.2+0.1=1 $$

Therefore, this is a valid BPA.

Notice that:

$$ m(\{A,B\})=0.2 $$

does not mean that:

$$ m(A)=0.1,\quad m(B)=0.1 $$

Instead, it means:

20% of the evidence supports the proposition that the answer is either A or B.

8. Belief Function

The Belief function measures the amount of evidence that definitely supports a proposition.

It is represented as:

$$ Bel(A) $$

The formula is:

$$ Bel(A)=\sum_{B\subseteq A}m(B) $$

In simple words:

Belief is the sum of all basic probability assignments that are completely contained within the proposition.

9. Example of Belief

Suppose:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B,C\} $$

and:

$$ m(A)=0.4 $$ $$ m(B)=0.2 $$ $$ m(A,B)=0.3 $$ $$ m(A,B,C)=0.1 $$

Find:

$$ Bel(A) $$

Only the subset \(\{A\}\) is completely contained in \(A\).

Therefore:

$$ Bel(A)=m(A) $$ $$ \boxed{Bel(A)=0.4} $$
10. Another Example

Find:

$$ Bel(\{A,B\}) $$

We consider all subsets of \(\{A,B\}\):

$$ \{A\},\{B\},\{A,B\} $$

Therefore:

$$ Bel(A,B)=m(A)+m(B)+m(A,B) $$ $$ =0.4+0.2+0.3 $$ $$ \boxed{Bel(A,B)=0.9} $$
11. Plausibility

The second important measure is Plausibility.

It is represented by:

$$ Pl(A) $$

Plausibility measures:

How much evidence does not contradict the proposition?

The formula is:

$$ Pl(A)=\sum_{B\cap A\neq\emptyset}m(B) $$

Therefore:

Belief = evidence that definitely supports \(A\)
Plausibility = evidence that could potentially support \(A\)
12. Belief and Plausibility Interval

One of the most important ideas in DST is:

$$ \boxed{Bel(A)\leq P(A)\leq Pl(A)} $$

DST therefore provides an interval rather than necessarily a single probability value.

The interval is:

$$ [Bel(A),Pl(A)] $$

This represents uncertainty about \(A\).

13. Example of Belief and Plausibility

Suppose:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B,C\} $$

and:

Evidence	BPA
\(A\)	0.4
\(B\)	0.2
\(A,B\)	0.3
\(A,B,C\)	0.1

Find \(Bel(A)\) and \(Pl(A)\).

Belief

Only \(A\) is completely contained in \(A\):

$$ Bel(A)=0.4 $$
Plausibility

We include every set that intersects \(A\):

\(A\) → 0.4
\(A,B\) → 0.3
\(A,B,C\) → 0.1

Therefore:

$$ Pl(A)=0.4+0.3+0.1 $$ $$ Pl(A)=0.8 $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{Bel(A)=0.4} $$ $$ \boxed{Pl(A)=0.8} $$

The probability of \(A\), if interpreted probabilistically, lies within:

$$ \boxed{0.4\leq P(A)\leq0.8} $$
14. Meaning of the Interval

The interval:

$$ [0.4,0.8] $$

can be interpreted as:

40% is the evidence definitely supporting A.
An additional 40% could support A but is not specific enough to say so.
Therefore A could have support as high as 80%.

This allows DST to represent ignorance explicitly.

15. Difference Between Probability and DST
Probability Theory	Dempster–Shafer Theory
Assigns probability to individual events	Assigns belief to sets of hypotheses
Usually gives one probability value	Can give an interval
Difficulty representing ignorance explicitly	Represents ignorance naturally
\(P(A)=0.6\)	\(Bel(A)=0.4,\ Pl(A)=0.8\)
Probability distributed among alternatives	Evidence can be assigned to groups
Useful for stochastic uncertainty	Useful for evidence-based uncertainty
16. Dempster's Rule of Combination

A major advantage of DST is the ability to combine evidence from multiple independent sources.

Suppose:

Sensor 1 provides evidence.
Sensor 2 provides additional evidence.

We can combine them using Dempster's Rule of Combination.

Let:

$$ m_1 $$

be the BPA from source 1, and

$$ m_2 $$

be the BPA from source 2.

The combined BPA is:

$$ m(A)= \frac{ \sum_{B\cap C=A}m_1(B)m_2(C) }{ 1-K } $$

where \(K\) represents the conflict between the evidence sources.

17. Conflict

Conflict occurs when two evidence sources support mutually exclusive hypotheses.

For example:

Sensor 1:

$$ m_1(A)=0.8 $$

Sensor 2:

$$ m_2(B)=0.7 $$

where:

$$ A\cap B=\emptyset $$

The evidence is conflicting.

The conflict coefficient is:

$$ K=\sum_{B\cap C=\emptyset}m_1(B)m_2(C) $$

The normalization factor is:

$$ 1-K $$
18. Simple Dempster Combination Example

Suppose:

$$ \Theta=\{A,B\} $$

Source 1:

$$ m_1(A)=0.6 $$ $$ m_1(B)=0.4 $$

Source 2:

$$ m_2(A)=0.7 $$ $$ m_2(B)=0.3 $$
Step 1: Calculate support for A
$$ m_1(A)m_2(A) $$ $$ =0.6\times0.7 $$ $$ =0.42 $$
Step 2: Calculate support for B
$$ m_1(B)m_2(B) $$ $$ =0.4\times0.3 $$ $$ =0.12 $$
Step 3: Calculate conflict

Conflicting combinations are:

$$ A\cap B=\emptyset $$

and

$$ B\cap A=\emptyset $$

Therefore:

$$ K=(0.6)(0.3)+(0.4)(0.7) $$ $$ K=0.18+0.28 $$ $$ K=0.46 $$

Thus:

$$ 1-K=0.54 $$
Step 4: Normalize
$$ m(A)=\frac{0.42}{0.54} $$ $$ \boxed{m(A)\approx0.778} $$

Similarly:

$$ m(B)=\frac{0.12}{0.54} $$ $$ \boxed{m(B)\approx0.222} $$

So the combined evidence strongly supports \(A\).

19. Open World vs Closed World

DST can also model situations where our knowledge is incomplete.

For example:

$$ \Theta=\{Car,Truck\} $$

Suppose:

$$ m(\{Car,Truck\})=1 $$

This means:

We know the object is either a car or truck, but we have no evidence to distinguish them.

This is different from saying:

$$ P(Car)=0.5,\quad P(Truck)=0.5 $$

The first expresses ignorance.

The second expresses specific probabilistic belief.

This distinction is very important.

20. Important Terms

For examination purposes, remember these terms:

1. Frame of Discernment
$$ \Theta $$

Set of all possible hypotheses.

2. Power Set
$$ 2^\Theta $$

Set of all subsets of the frame.

3. Basic Probability Assignment
$$ m(A) $$

Amount of evidence assigned directly to \(A\).

4. Belief
$$ Bel(A) $$

Evidence that definitely supports \(A\).

5. Plausibility
$$ Pl(A) $$

Evidence that does not contradict \(A\).

6. Conflict
$$ K $$

Amount of evidence from different sources supporting mutually exclusive hypotheses.

7. Dempster's Rule

Method for combining evidence from multiple sources.

21. Relationship Between Belief and Plausibility

An important relationship is:

$$ \boxed{Bel(A)\leq Pl(A)} $$

Also:

$$ \boxed{Pl(A)=1-Bel(\neg A)} $$

where \(\neg A\) represents the complement of \(A\).

Therefore:

$$ Bel(A)\leq P(A)\leq Pl(A) $$

The difference:

$$ Pl(A)-Bel(A) $$

can be considered a measure of uncertainty/ignorance associated with \(A\).

24. Limitations
Limitations of DST
Calculations become expensive as the number of hypotheses increases.
The power set contains:
$$ 2^n $$

subsets for \(n\) hypotheses.

Dempster's rule can behave unexpectedly when evidence sources have very high conflict.
Combining many sources can become computationally expensive.
Assigning appropriate BPA values can itself be difficult.

26. Complete Example

Suppose an autonomous vehicle needs to classify an object.

Possible hypotheses:

$$ \Theta=\{Car,Truck\} $$

Camera evidence:

$$ m_1(Car)=0.6 $$ $$ m_1(Car,Truck)=0.4 $$

Radar evidence:

$$ m_2(Car)=0.7 $$ $$ m_2(Truck)=0.1 $$ $$ m_2(Car,Truck)=0.2 $$

The system combines the evidence using Dempster's Rule.

The result gives a new BPA distribution.

Then the system calculates:

$$ Bel(Car) $$

and:

$$ Pl(Car) $$

If the resulting belief/plausibility interval strongly favors Car, the vehicle can classify the object as a car.

This demonstrates the complete DST reasoning process:

$$ \boxed{ Evidence \rightarrow BPA \rightarrow Combination \rightarrow Belief/Plausibility \rightarrow Decision } $$
27. Key Difference: Uncertainty vs Ignorance

This is a particularly important concept for students.

Probability

If:

$$ P(A)=0.5,\quad P(B)=0.5 $$

we are saying:

We believe A and B each have 50% probability.

DST

If:

$$ m(\{A,B\})=1 $$

we are saying:

We only know that the answer is A or B. We don't know enough to distinguish them.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{\text{Probability expresses uncertainty}} $$

while DST can additionally express:

$$ \boxed{\text{ignorance about the exact hypothesis}} $$

What is \(\Theta\)?

For example, if an AI system is identifying a vehicle:

$$ \Theta=\{Car, Truck, Bus\} $$

Here:

Car = one hypothesis
Truck = one hypothesis

\(\Theta\) = the complete set of possible hypotheses
Why is \(\Theta\) important?

DST assigns evidence not only to individual hypotheses but also to subsets of \(\Theta\).

For example:

$$ m(\{Car\})=0.5 $$

means 50% evidence specifically supports Car.

But:

$$ m(\{Car,Truck\})=0.3 $$

means 30% evidence says the object is either Car or Truck, but we don't have enough information to distinguish between them.

$$ m(\Theta)=0.2 $$

means 20% of the evidence is completely uncommitted—we only know that the answer is somewhere within the possible hypotheses.

$$ 2^\Theta= \{\emptyset,\{A\},\{B\},\{C\},\{A,B\},\{A,C\},\{B,C\},\{A,B,C\}\} $$

$$ \boxed{\Theta=\text{Frame of Discernment}} $$ $$ \boxed{2^\Theta=\text{Power Set}} $$ $$ \boxed{m(A)=\text{Basic Probability/Belief Assignment}} $$
