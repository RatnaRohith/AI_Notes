# REVIEW OF PROBABILITY

## 1. Introduction to Probability

Probability is a branch of mathematics that measures the likelihood of an event occurring.

In Artificial Intelligence (AI), probability is used to represent uncertainty and make decisions when complete information is not available.

Example:
An AI weather prediction system may estimate a 70% probability of rain tomorrow.

## 2. Basic Terminology

### 2.1 Experiment

An experiment is an action or process that produces an outcome.

Examples:
- Tossing a coin.
- Rolling a die.
- Drawing a card from a deck.

### 2.2 Sample Space

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

It is represented by S.

Example 1: Tossing a coin

S = {H, T}

Example 2: Rolling a die

S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

### 2.3 Event

An event is a subset of the sample space.

Example:

When rolling a die, let A be the event of obtaining an even number.

A = {2, 4, 6}

## 3. Probability of an Event

When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event A is:

P(A) = Number of favorable outcomes / Total number of possible outcomes

Example 1: Probability of getting a 4 on a fair die.

P(4) = 1/6

Example 2: Probability of getting an even number.

P(E) = 3/6 = 1/2

## 4. Range of Probability

The probability of any event A satisfies:

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

Interpretation:

- P(A) = 0: Impossible event.
- P(A) = 1: Certain event.
- 0 < P(A) < 1: Uncertain event.

Probability can also be expressed as a percentage.

Example:

P(A) = 0.75 = 75%

## 5. Complement of an Event

The complement of event A represents the event that A does not occur.

It is represented by Aᶜ or ¬A.

Formula:

P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)

Example:

If the probability of rain is 0.3, then:

P(Rain) = 0.3

P(No Rain) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7

Therefore, the probability of no rain is 0.7 or 70%.

## 6. Addition Rule of Probability

The addition rule calculates the probability that event A or event B occurs.

Formula:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Here:

- P(A ∪ B) represents the probability of A or B occurring.
- P(A ∩ B) represents the probability of both A and B occurring.

The intersection is subtracted because it is counted twice when adding P(A) and P(B).

### 6.1 Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur simultaneously.

For mutually exclusive events:

P(A ∩ B) = 0

Therefore:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

Example:

When rolling a die:

A = Getting a 2

B = Getting a 4

P(A ∪ B) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3

## 7. Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring when another event is already known to have occurred.

It is represented by P(A | B).

Formula:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Condition: P(B) > 0

Here:

- P(A | B) means the probability of A given B.
- P(A ∩ B) means the probability of both A and B.
- P(B) means the probability of B.

Example:

Suppose:

P(AI) = 0.60

P(AI ∩ ML) = 0.30

The probability that a student studies Machine Learning given that the student studies AI is:

P(ML | AI) = P(ML ∩ AI) / P(AI)

P(ML | AI) = 0.30 / 0.60

P(ML | AI) = 0.50

Therefore, the conditional probability is 50%.

## 8. Independent Events

Two events are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

For independent events A and B:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

Also:

P(A | B) = P(A)

Example:

Tossing a fair coin twice.

The result of the first toss does not affect the second toss.

Probability of getting heads on the first toss:

P(H₁) = 1/2

Probability of getting heads on the second toss:

P(H₂) = 1/2

Probability of getting heads on both tosses:

P(H₁ ∩ H₂) = P(H₁) × P(H₂)

P(H₁ ∩ H₂) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

## 9. Multiplication Rule of Probability

The multiplication rule calculates the probability that two events occur together.

Formula:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B)

Similarly:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) × P(A)

This rule is useful when the events are dependent.

For independent events:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

This concept is important in AI systems that calculate the joint probability of multiple events.

## 10. Random Variables

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each possible outcome of a random experiment.

It is commonly represented by X.

Example: Coin Toss

Define X as follows:

X = 1 if the outcome is Heads.

X = 0 if the outcome is Tails.

Here, X is a random variable.

### 10.1 Types of Random Variables

Discrete Random Variable:

A random variable that takes countable values.

Examples:
- Number of heads in coin tosses.
- Number of customers visiting a shop.
- Outcome of rolling a die.

Continuous Random Variable:

A random variable that can take any value within a specified interval.

Examples:
- Temperature.
- Height.
- Time.
- Speed.

## 11. Probability Distribution

A probability distribution describes the probabilities associated with the possible values of a random variable.

Example: Rolling a Fair Die

X       P(X)

1       1/6

2       1/6

3       1/6

4       1/6

5       1/6

6       1/6

The sum of all probabilities in a discrete probability distribution must equal 1.

Σ P(X = x) = 1

Here, Σ represents summation over all possible values of X.

## 12. Joint Probability

Joint probability represents the probability that two or more events occur together.

It is represented by:

P(A, B) = P(A ∩ B)

Example:

P(Rain, Traffic)

This represents the probability that it is raining and there is heavy traffic.

Joint probability is important in Bayesian networks, where multiple variables may be related.

## 13. Marginal Probability

Marginal probability is the probability of one variable without specifying the value of another variable.

Example:

P(Rain) represents the probability of rain regardless of whether traffic is heavy or light.

If T represents traffic conditions, then:

P(Rain) = Σₜ P(Rain, T = t)

This means adding the joint probabilities over all possible traffic conditions.

The process of obtaining a marginal probability from a joint probability distribution is called marginalization.

## 14. Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem is a fundamental probability rule used to update beliefs when new evidence becomes available.

Formula:

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)

Where:

P(A | B) = Posterior Probability

The updated probability of A after observing B.

P(B | A) = Likelihood

The probability of observing B when A is true.

P(A) = Prior Probability

The initial probability of A before observing B.

P(B) = Evidence

The overall probability of observing B.

### Example: Medical Diagnosis

Suppose an AI medical diagnosis system wants to calculate the probability that a patient has a disease given a positive test result.

The required probability is:

P(Disease | Positive)

Using Bayes' theorem:

P(Disease | Positive) = [P(Positive | Disease) × P(Disease)] / P(Positive)

The system uses the disease's prior probability, the test's likelihood, and the overall probability of a positive result to calculate the posterior probability.

Bayes' theorem is widely used in probabilistic reasoning and AI diagnosis systems.

## 15. Expected Value

Expected value is the weighted average of the possible values of a random variable, where each value is weighted by its probability.

For a discrete random variable:

E(X) = Σₓ x × P(X = x)

Example: Rolling a Fair Die

E(X) = 1 × 1/6 + 2 × 1/6 + 3 × 1/6 + 4 × 1/6 + 5 × 1/6 + 6 × 1/6

E(X) = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6

E(X) = 21/6 = 3.5

Therefore, the expected value is 3.5.

Note: A single die roll cannot produce 3.5. It is the long-run average over many rolls.

## 16. Variance and Standard Deviation

### 16.1 Variance

Variance measures how much the values of a random variable vary around their expected value.

Formula:

Var(X) = E[(X − E(X))²]

An equivalent formula is:

Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]²

A larger variance indicates greater spread in the possible values.

### 16.2 Standard Deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance.

σ = √Var(X)

It measures the spread of values in the same units as the random variable.

Variance and standard deviation help describe uncertainty in data and probabilistic models.

## 17. Important Probability Concepts in AI

1. Probability:
   Represents uncertainty about events.

2. Conditional Probability:
   Calculates probabilities using known evidence.

3. Joint Probability:
   Represents the probability of events occurring together.

4. Marginal Probability:
   Calculates the probability of a variable independently of other variables.

5. Independence:
   Identifies events whose probabilities do not affect each other.

6. Bayes' Theorem:
   Updates beliefs using new evidence.

7. Random Variables:
   Represent uncertain quantities numerically.

8. Probability Distribution:
   Describes the possible values and their probabilities.

9. Expected Value:
   Supports decisions based on average expected outcomes.

10. Variance:
    Measures the spread of possible outcomes.

## 18. Applications of Probability in Artificial Intelligence

Traditional Boolean logic represents statements as True or False.

Probabilistic reasoning allows an AI system to represent uncertainty about whether a statement is true.

Example:

Logic-based statement:

If smoke is detected, there is a fire.

Probabilistic statement:

If smoke is detected, the probability of a fire may be 80%, depending on the available evidence.

Probability is useful in AI because real-world systems often deal with:

- Incomplete information.
- Noisy sensor readings.
- Uncertain observations.
- Ambiguous situations.
- Unreliable data.
- Changing environments.

Applications include:

- Medical diagnosis.
- Weather forecasting.
- Bayesian networks.
- Spam email classification.
- Speech recognition.
- Robotics and sensor fusion.
- Decision-making under uncertainty.

## 19. Important Probability Formulas

1. Range of Probability:

   0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

2. Complement Rule:

   P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)

3. Addition Rule:

   P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

4. Conditional Probability:

   P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

5. Multiplication Rule:

   P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B)

6. Independent Events:

   P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

7. Bayes' Theorem:

   P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)

8. Expected Value:

   E(X) = Σₓ x × P(X = x)

9. Variance:

   Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]²

10. Standard Deviation:

    σ = √Var(X)

## 20. Conclusion

Probability provides the mathematical foundation for reasoning under uncertainty.

The concepts of conditional probability, joint probability, marginal probability, independence, and Bayes' theorem help AI systems interpret evidence and update beliefs.

Expected value and variance help describe outcomes and uncertainty.

These concepts form the foundation for Bayesian reasoning, Bayesian networks, probabilistic inference, and decision-making under uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence.
