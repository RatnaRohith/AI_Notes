# KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION IN ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge Representation (KR) is the process of representing real-world knowledge in a structured form that a computer system can store, process, and use for reasoning.

An AI system requires knowledge about objects, properties, relationships, facts, rules, events, and situations to solve problems and make decisions.

Definition:

Knowledge Representation is the method of encoding knowledge about the world into a formal structure that can be processed and reasoned about by an AI system.

Example:

Knowledge:
- All humans are mortal.
- Socrates is a human.

Inference:
- Socrates is mortal.

Logical Representation:

∀x (Human(x) → Mortal(x))

Human(Socrates)

Therefore:

Mortal(Socrates)

--------------------------------------------------

## 2. Need for Knowledge Representation

Knowledge Representation is important because an AI system must organize knowledge in a form that supports intelligent behavior.

The main purposes are:

1. To represent facts about the world.
2. To describe objects and their properties.
3. To represent relationships between objects.
4. To store rules and constraints.
5. To derive new knowledge from existing knowledge.
6. To solve problems using stored information.
7. To support decision making and planning.
8. To represent incomplete or uncertain information.

Example:

Fact:
Ravi is a student.

Rule:
Every student has a student ID.

Conclusion:
Ravi has a student ID.

The system uses the stored fact and rule to derive a conclusion.

--------------------------------------------------

## 3. Knowledge Representation Cycle

The knowledge representation process can be illustrated as follows:

Real World
    ↓
Knowledge Acquisition
    ↓
Knowledge Representation
    ↓
Knowledge Base
    ↓
Reasoning and Inference
    ↓
New Knowledge
    ↓
Decision or Action

Explanation:

1. Knowledge Acquisition:
   Collect information from experts, documents, observations, or other sources.

2. Knowledge Representation:
   Convert the collected information into a suitable structured form.

3. Knowledge Base:
   Store facts, rules, relationships, and other knowledge.

4. Reasoning and Inference:
   Apply reasoning techniques to derive conclusions.

5. Decision or Action:
   Use the results to solve a problem or perform a task.

--------------------------------------------------

## 4. Representational Adequacy

Definition:

Representational adequacy is the ability of a knowledge representation system to represent the types of knowledge required for a particular problem.

The system should be able to represent:

- Objects
- Properties
- Relationships
- Facts
- Rules
- Events
- Actions
- Situations

Example:

Statement:
Ravi owns a car.

Representation:

Owns(Ravi, Car1)

Statement:
The car is red.

Representation:

Color(Car1, Red)

A representation is adequate when it can express the knowledge needed by the application.

Key Point:

Representational Adequacy answers the question:

"Can the system represent the required knowledge?"

--------------------------------------------------

## 5. Inferential Adequacy

Definition:

Inferential adequacy is the ability of a knowledge representation system to derive new knowledge from existing facts and rules.

Example:

Fact:

Human(Ravi)

Rule:

∀x (Human(x) → Mortal(x))

Conclusion:

Mortal(Ravi)

The system uses the general rule and the specific fact to derive a new conclusion.

Key Point:

Inferential Adequacy answers the question:

"Can the system derive new knowledge from existing knowledge?"

--------------------------------------------------

## 6. Inferential Efficiency

Definition:

Inferential efficiency refers to the ability of a knowledge representation system to perform reasoning with minimum unnecessary computation.

A large knowledge base may contain millions of facts and thousands of rules. Examining every fact for every query can be inefficient.

Methods used to improve inferential efficiency include:

1. Indexing
2. Heuristics
3. Efficient search algorithms
4. Rule organization
5. Inference optimization

Example:

Question:
Can Ravi drive a car?

The system should focus on relevant information about Ravi, driving, licences, and cars rather than examining unrelated facts.

Key Point:

Inferential Efficiency answers the question:

"Can the system reason efficiently?"

--------------------------------------------------

## 7. Acquisitional Efficiency

Definition:

Acquisitional efficiency is the ability to add, modify, and maintain knowledge in a knowledge base easily and efficiently.

Knowledge may change over time, so an AI system should support:

1. Adding new facts.
2. Modifying existing facts.
3. Removing outdated facts.
4. Updating rules.
5. Incorporating knowledge from new sources.

Example:

Old Knowledge:
Ravi works at Company A.

Updated Knowledge:
Ravi works at Company B.

The knowledge base should be updated appropriately.

Key Point:

Acquisitional Efficiency answers the question:

"Can the system acquire and update knowledge easily?"

--------------------------------------------------

## 8. Handling Incomplete Knowledge

Definition:

Incomplete knowledge occurs when an AI system does not have all the information required to determine a situation completely.

Example:

Known fact:

Bird(Tweety)

Unknown information:

Color(Tweety, ?)

The system knows that Tweety is a bird but does not know its color.

Important distinction:

- TRUE: The statement is known to be true.
- FALSE: The statement is known to be false.
- UNKNOWN: The available knowledge does not establish whether the statement is true or false.

Example:

The system does not know whether Ravi owns a car.

It should not automatically conclude that Ravi does not own a car.

Key Point:

Unknown does not necessarily mean false.

--------------------------------------------------

## 9. Handling Uncertain Knowledge

Definition:

Uncertain knowledge is information whose truth or reliability is not known with complete certainty.

Example:

A doctor suspects that a patient may have influenza.

This is a possibility, not a confirmed diagnosis.

Methods for representing uncertainty include:

1. Probability Theory
2. Bayesian Networks
3. Fuzzy Logic
4. Certainty Factors
5. Possibility Theory

Example:

Probability of rain = 0.7

This represents a 70% probability of rain, assuming the probability model is appropriately defined.

Key Point:

An AI system should distinguish between certain facts and uncertain conclusions.

--------------------------------------------------

## 10. Representing Common-Sense Knowledge

Definition:

Common-sense knowledge refers to everyday knowledge that humans generally understand through ordinary experience.

Examples:

- Unsupported objects may fall because of gravity.
- Fire can cause burns.
- People need food and water.
- A glass dropped onto a hard floor may break.

Problem:

Humans use a vast amount of common-sense knowledge without explicitly stating it.

Representing all this knowledge in a computer system is difficult.

AI systems must often use general rules, contextual information, and reasoning to handle common-sense situations.

--------------------------------------------------

## 11. Representing Time

Definition:

Temporal representation is the representation of facts, events, and relationships involving time.

Examples of temporal relationships:

- Before
- After
- During
- Since
- Until
- Duration

Example:

Event A occurs before Event B.

Representation:

Before(A, B)

Another example:

Student(Ravi, 2026)

Graduate(Ravi, 2029)

These statements represent different states or roles at different times.

Temporal representation is useful in scheduling, planning, event analysis, and robotics.

--------------------------------------------------

## 12. Representing Change

Definition:

Representing change involves describing how the state or properties of an object change after an event or action.

Example:

Initial State:

Closed(Door)

Action:

OpenDoor()

New State:

Open(Door)

General process:

Initial State
    ↓
Action
    ↓
New State

AI systems must also determine which properties change and which properties remain unchanged after an action.

This is important in:

- Robotics
- Automated Planning
- Game AI
- Simulation Systems
- Autonomous Vehicles

--------------------------------------------------

## 13. Handling Consistency

Definition:

Consistency refers to the absence of incompatible or contradictory information within a knowledge base, according to its reasoning rules.

Example:

CanFly(Tweety)

CannotFly(Tweety)

These statements conflict if both refer to the same conditions and meaning.

Possible approaches include:

1. Detecting contradictions.
2. Checking the reliability of information sources.
3. Updating outdated facts.
4. Resolving conflicts using predefined rules.
5. Using reasoning methods designed to tolerate contradictions.

Key Point:

A knowledge representation system should identify and appropriately handle conflicting information.

--------------------------------------------------

## 14. Scalability

Definition:

Scalability is the ability of a knowledge representation system to handle increasing amounts of knowledge without unacceptable degradation in performance.

A small knowledge base may contain only hundreds of facts, while a large system may contain millions or billions of records.

A scalable system should support:

1. Efficient storage.
2. Fast information retrieval.
3. Efficient reasoning.
4. Knowledge updates.
5. Management of relationships between large numbers of entities.

Example:

A search engine must organize and retrieve information from an enormous collection of web pages.

--------------------------------------------------

## 15. Granularity of Knowledge

Definition:

Granularity refers to the level of detail used when representing knowledge.

Example:

Simple representation:

Drove(Ravi, College)

Detailed representation:

Driver = Ravi
Vehicle = Car1
StartingLocation = Home
Destination = College
Time = 8:00 AM
Speed = 40 km/h
Weather = Rainy

The detailed representation provides more information but requires additional storage and processing.

Therefore, an appropriate level of detail should be selected based on the problem.

--------------------------------------------------

## 16. Knowledge Representation Techniques

Different techniques are suitable for different kinds of knowledge.

### 16.1 Logic

Used to represent facts and formal relationships.

Example:

Human(Ravi)

∀x (Human(x) → Mortal(x))

### 16.2 Production Rules

Used to represent knowledge in IF–THEN form.

Example:

IF Rain
THEN Carry(Umbrella)

### 16.3 Semantic Networks

Represent concepts and relationships as a network.

Example:

Canary → IS-A → Bird

Bird → HAS → Wings

### 16.4 Frames

Represent structured knowledge about objects or concepts using attributes and values.

Example:

Frame: Car

Color = Red
Wheels = 4
FuelType = Petrol

### 16.5 Ontologies

Represent concepts, categories, properties, and relationships within a domain.

Example:

Vehicle
    ├── Car
    ├── Bus
    └── Motorcycle

### 16.6 Probabilistic Representations

Represent uncertain relationships using probabilities.

Example:

Probability of Rain = 0.7

--------------------------------------------------

## 17. Trade-Off Between Expressiveness and Efficiency

A knowledge representation system must balance expressiveness and computational efficiency.

Expressiveness:
The ability to represent complex facts, relationships, rules, and situations.

Efficiency:
The ability to store, retrieve, and reason about knowledge using acceptable computational resources.

A highly expressive representation may support complex reasoning but require more computational effort.

A simpler representation may be faster but unable to express some complex situations.

Therefore, the choice of representation depends on the application's requirements.
