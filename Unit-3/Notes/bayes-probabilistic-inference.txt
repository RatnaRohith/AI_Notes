BAYES PROBABILISTIC INFERENCE
================================

1. INTRODUCTION

In Artificial Intelligence (AI), an agent often has to make decisions when it does not have complete or certain information.

Examples:
- A patient has symptoms. Does the patient have a disease?
- A sensor gives a reading. Is the machine faulty?
- An email contains certain words. Is it spam?
- A robot observes an object. Is it an obstacle?

In such situations, probabilistic inference allows an AI system to reason about uncertainty.

Definition:
Bayesian inference is the process of updating the probability of a hypothesis when new evidence becomes available.

The system does not simply ask, "Is the patient sick?"
Instead, it asks, "How likely is it that the patient is sick, given the available evidence?"


2. WHAT IS PROBABILISTIC INFERENCE?

Probabilistic inference is the process of calculating the probability of an unknown event or hypothesis based on available information and evidence.

Example:

D = The patient has a disease.
T = The patient has a positive test result.

We want to calculate:

P(D | T)

Read as: Probability of D given T.

It means the probability that a patient has the disease, given that the test result is positive.

Probabilistic inference is useful when information is incomplete, uncertain, or unreliable.


3. BAYES' THEOREM

Bayes' theorem provides a mathematical method for updating beliefs when new evidence is observed.

Formula:

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)

Where:

P(A | B) = Posterior probability
P(B | A) = Likelihood
P(A)     = Prior probability
P(B)     = Evidence probability

The formula can be remembered as:

Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence


4. UNDERSTANDING THE FOUR TERMS

4.1. PRIOR PROBABILITY — P(A)

The prior probability is our initial belief about an event before observing new evidence.

Example:

If 1% of people have a particular disease:

P(D) = 0.01 = 1%

This is the prior probability of the disease.

4.2. LIKELIHOOD — P(B | A)

Likelihood tells us how probable the evidence is when the hypothesis is true.

Example:

If a person has the disease, there is a 90% chance that the medical test is positive.

P(T | D) = 0.90 = 90%

4.3. EVIDENCE PROBABILITY — P(B)

Evidence probability is the overall probability of observing the evidence.

Example:

P(T) = Probability that a randomly selected person receives a positive test result.

It includes positive results from people who have the disease and people who do not have the disease.

4.4. POSTERIOR PROBABILITY — P(A | B)

The posterior probability is the updated probability after observing evidence.

Example:

P(D | T) = Probability that the patient has the disease, given a positive test result.

The posterior probability is generally the value we want to calculate using Bayes' theorem.


5. BAYESIAN INFERENCE PROCESS

The general process is:

Prior Belief
     |
     v
Observe New Evidence
     |
     v
Apply Bayes' Theorem
     |
     v
Calculate Posterior Probability
     |
     v
Update Belief
     |
     v
Make an AI Decision

Example:

Initial belief: A patient has a 1% chance of having a disease.
New evidence: The medical test is positive.
Bayesian inference: Calculate the probability of disease given the positive result.
Result: Obtain an updated probability.


6. WORKED EXAMPLE — MEDICAL DIAGNOSIS

Problem:

Suppose the following information is available:

1. Probability of having the disease = 1%.
2. Probability of a positive test when the disease exists = 90%.
3. Probability of a positive test when the disease does not exist = 5%.

Given that the test is positive, calculate the probability that the person actually has the disease.

Given:

P(D) = 0.01
P(T | D) = 0.90
P(T | ¬D) = 0.05

Here:

D  = Person has the disease.
¬D = Person does not have the disease.
T  = Test result is positive.

We need to calculate:

P(D | T)


STEP 1: CALCULATE THE COMPLEMENT

The probability that a person does not have the disease is:

P(¬D) = 1 − P(D)

P(¬D) = 1 − 0.01

P(¬D) = 0.99


STEP 2: CALCULATE THE OVERALL PROBABILITY OF A POSITIVE TEST

Using the law of total probability:

P(T) = P(T | D) × P(D)
       + P(T | ¬D) × P(¬D)

Substitute the values:

P(T) = (0.90 × 0.01) + (0.05 × 0.99)

P(T) = 0.009 + 0.0495

P(T) = 0.0585


STEP 3: APPLY BAYES' THEOREM

Bayes' theorem:

P(D | T) = [P(T | D) × P(D)] / P(T)

Substitute the values:

P(D | T) = (0.90 × 0.01) / 0.0585

P(D | T) = 0.009 / 0.0585

P(D | T) ≈ 0.1538

Convert the probability into a percentage:

P(D | T) ≈ 15.38%


FINAL ANSWER:

The probability that the person actually has the disease, given a positive test result, is approximately 15.38%.


7. WHY IS THE PROBABILITY ONLY 15.38%?

The reason is that the disease is relatively rare.

Only 1% of the population has the disease.

Consider 10,000 people.

A. PEOPLE WHO HAVE THE DISEASE

Number of people with the disease:

10,000 × 0.01 = 100

If 90% of these people test positive:

100 × 0.90 = 90

Therefore, 90 people have the disease and test positive.

B. PEOPLE WHO DO NOT HAVE THE DISEASE

Number of people without the disease:

10,000 − 100 = 9,900

If 5% of these people test positive:

9,900 × 0.05 = 495

Therefore, 495 people do not have the disease but test positive.

C. TOTAL POSITIVE TEST RESULTS

Total positive results:

90 + 495 = 585

D. FINAL PROBABILITY

Among 585 people with positive results, only 90 actually have the disease.

P(D | T) = 90 / 585

P(D | T) ≈ 0.1538

P(D | T) ≈ 15.38%

Important observation:

A positive test does not automatically mean that the person has the disease.

Bayesian inference considers both the test accuracy and the prior probability of the disease.

9. BAYESIAN NETWORKS

A Bayesian Network is a graphical model used to represent probabilistic relationships between variables.

Its main components are:

1. Nodes:
   Represent random variables.

2. Directed edges:
   Represent direct probabilistic dependencies between variables.

3. Conditional Probability Tables (CPTs):
   Represent the probability distribution of a variable given its parent variables.

Example:

        Rain
          |
          v
       Wet Road
          |
          v
       Accident

Explanation:

- Rain influences the probability that the road becomes wet.
- A wet road may influence the probability of an accident.

This is a simplified illustrative network. Real-world models may contain additional variables and dependencies.

A Bayesian Network allows an AI system to reason about uncertain events and update probabilities when evidence becomes available.


10. CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring, given that another event has occurred.

Notation:

P(A | B)

Read as: Probability of A given B.

Formula:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Where:

P(A | B) = Probability of A given B.
P(A ∩ B) = Probability that both A and B occur.
P(B)     = Probability of B.

This formula applies when P(B) > 0.

Example:

P(Rain | Cloudy)

This represents the probability of rain, given that the sky is cloudy.


11. DERIVATION OF BAYES' THEOREM

Bayes' theorem can be derived from conditional probability.

Step 1:

From the definition of conditional probability:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Step 2:

Similarly:

P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A)

Rearranging:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) × P(A)

Step 3:

Substitute this expression into the first equation:

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)

Therefore:

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)

This is Bayes' theorem.


12. BAYES' THEOREM TERMINOLOGY

Prior Probability — P(A):
The initial probability of a hypothesis before observing new evidence.

Likelihood — P(B | A):
The probability of observing the evidence if the hypothesis is true.

Evidence Probability — P(B):
The overall probability of observing the evidence.

Posterior Probability — P(A | B):
The updated probability of the hypothesis after observing the evidence.

Memory trick:

Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence


13. BAYESIAN INFERENCE VS CLASSICAL LOGIC

CLASSICAL LOGIC:

- Deals with truth and falsity.
- Often represents conclusions as true or false.
- Uses facts and logical rules.
- Example: If A implies B and A is true, then B is true.

BAYESIAN INFERENCE:

- Deals with uncertainty.
- Represents beliefs using probabilities.
- Updates beliefs using evidence.
- Example: If P(B | A) = 0.80, then B has an 80% conditional probability given A.

Example:

Classical reasoning:

Rule: If it rains, the road becomes wet.
Fact: It is raining.
Conclusion: The road is wet.

Probabilistic reasoning:

Suppose the probability that the road is wet when it rains is 80%.

P(Wet Road | Rain) = 0.80

Given that it is raining, the probability of a wet road is 80%, under the specified model.

Note:
Bayesian inference and classical logic are not mutually exclusive. An AI system can use both logical rules and probabilistic reasoning.


14. BAYESIAN UPDATING

Bayesian inference updates a belief when new evidence becomes available.

Suppose an AI system initially estimates:

P(A) = 0.30 = 30%

After observing evidence B, it calculates:

P(A | B) = 0.75 = 75%

The belief changes from 30% to 75%.

Process:

Initial Belief: 30%
        |
        v
New Evidence Observed
        |
        v
Bayesian Updating
        |
        v
Updated Belief: 75%

The values above are illustrative.

The main idea is that new evidence can increase or decrease the probability of a hypothesis.

16. NAÏVE BAYES CLASSIFIER

Naïve Bayes is a probabilistic classification algorithm based on Bayes' theorem.

It is called "naïve" because it assumes that features are conditionally independent of one another, given the class.

Suppose:

C = Class
X₁, X₂, ..., Xₙ = Features

The objective is to calculate:

P(C | X₁, X₂, ..., Xₙ)

Under the naïve conditional-independence assumption:

P(C | X₁, ..., Xₙ)
    ∝ P(C) × P(X₁ | C) × P(X₂ | C) × ... × P(Xₙ | C)

In product notation:

P(C | X₁, ..., Xₙ)
    ∝ P(C) × ∏ P(Xᵢ | C)

The classifier calculates a score or posterior probability for each class and selects the class with the highest posterior probability.

Applications:

- Email spam detection
- Sentiment analysis
- Document classification
- Text categorization
- Basic medical classification


17. EXAMPLE — SPAM CLASSIFICATION

Suppose an email contains the words:

"Win free money"

Features:

X₁ = "win"
X₂ = "free"
X₃ = "money"

Possible classes:

C₁ = Spam
C₂ = Not Spam

The classifier estimates:

P(Spam | Win, Free, Money)

and:

P(Not Spam | Win, Free, Money)

If:

P(Spam | Features) > P(Not Spam | Features)

the email is classified as Spam.

Otherwise, it is classified as Not Spam.

The actual result depends on the training data and estimated probabilities.

19. ADVANTAGES OF BAYESIAN INFERENCE

1. Handles uncertainty:
   It allows AI systems to reason when information is incomplete.

2. Incorporates prior knowledge:
   Previous knowledge can be represented using prior probabilities.

3. Updates beliefs:
   New evidence can change the probability of a hypothesis.

4. Supports decision-making:
   An AI agent can use updated probabilities to select actions.

5. Handles noisy information:
   Probabilistic models can account for unreliable observations and sensor errors.

6. Provides interpretable probabilities:
   The results can express the estimated likelihood of different hypotheses.


20. LIMITATIONS OF BAYESIAN INFERENCE

1. Requires probability information:
   Prior and conditional probabilities may be difficult to obtain.

2. Computational complexity:
   Large Bayesian models can require substantial computation.

3. Dependence assumptions:
   Simplified models, such as Naïve Bayes, assume conditional independence between features given the class. This assumption may not always hold.

4. Inaccurate probability estimates:
   Poor estimates of prior probabilities or likelihoods can lead to poor results.

5. Model limitations:
   A model that ignores important variables or relationships may produce misleading conclusions.

22. IMPORTANT FORMULAS

A. CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Condition: P(B) > 0.


B. JOINT PROBABILITY

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B)

Also:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) × P(A)


C. BAYES' THEOREM

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) × P(A)] / P(B)


D. COMPLEMENT RULE

P(¬A) = 1 − P(A)


E. LAW OF TOTAL PROBABILITY

P(B) = P(B | A) × P(A)
     + P(B | ¬A) × P(¬A)


F. NAÏVE BAYES CLASSIFICATION

P(C | X₁, ..., Xₙ)
    ∝ P(C) × ∏ P(Xᵢ | C)

The expression is proportional to the posterior probability. A normalized posterior requires dividing the class score by the total score across all classes, when the model and probabilities permit this calculation.

The central formula is:

Where:

H = Hypothesis
E = Evidence
P(H) = Prior probability
P(E | H) = Likelihood
P(E) = Evidence probability
P(H | E) = Posterior probability
