First-Order Logic (FOL) – Lecture
1. Introduction

First-Order Logic (FOL) is a formal language used in Artificial Intelligence to represent knowledge about objects, their properties, and relationships between objects.

It is more expressive than Propositional Logic because it can represent:

Individual objects
Properties of objects
Relationships between objects
General statements about groups of objects
Example

Consider the statements:

Socrates is a man.
All men are mortal.
Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

In propositional logic, these statements can be represented as separate propositions.

In FOL, we can represent the general rule:

$$ \forall x\; (Man(x) \rightarrow Mortal(x)) $$

and the fact:

$$ Man(Socrates) $$

From these, we can infer:

$$ Mortal(Socrates) $$
2. Why First-Order Logic?

Propositional logic has limitations.

For example:

Ram is a student.
Ravi is a student.
Sita is a student.

We need separate propositions:

$$ Student(Ram) $$ $$ Student(Ravi) $$ $$ Student(Sita) $$

But suppose we want to express:

Every student is a person.

We cannot easily express this general relationship using only propositional logic.

FOL allows us to write:

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Person(x)) $$

This single statement applies to every object in the domain.

3. Components of First-Order Logic

The major components of FOL are:

Constants
Variables
Predicates
Functions
Logical connectives
Quantifiers
4. Constants

A constant represents a particular object or individual.

Examples:

Ram
Sita
John
Delhi
Earth

For example:

$$ Student(Ram) $$

Here, Ram is a constant representing a particular person.

5. Variables

A variable represents an arbitrary object in the domain.

Common variables are:

$$ x,\ y,\ z $$

Example:

$$ Student(x) $$

means:

x is a student.

The variable x does not refer to one specific person until it is assigned or quantified.

6. Predicates

A predicate represents a property or relationship.

Unary predicate

A unary predicate has one argument.

$$ Student(x) $$

Meaning:

x is a student.

Other examples:

$$ Human(x) $$ $$ Tall(x) $$ $$ Intelligent(x) $$
Binary predicate

A binary predicate has two arguments and represents a relationship.

$$ Likes(x,y) $$

Meaning:

x likes y.

For example:

$$ Likes(Ram,Sita) $$

means:

Ram likes Sita.

Other examples:

$$ Father(Ram,Sita) $$ $$ Friend(Ravi,John) $$ $$ LivesIn(Ram,Hyderabad) $$
Ternary predicate

A ternary predicate has three arguments.

$$ Between(x,y,z) $$

For example:

$$ Between(A,B,C) $$

could mean:

B is between A and C.

7. Functions

A function maps one or more objects to another object.

Examples:

$$ Father(x) $$

means:

father of x.

$$ Mother(x) $$

means:

mother of x.

Another example:

$$ Age(x) $$

could represent the age associated with x.

Predicate vs Function
Predicate	Function
Represents property/relationship	Represents an object/value
Produces True/False	Produces an object/value
Student(Ram)	Father(Ram)
Likes(Ram,Sita)	Mother(Ram)
8. Logical Connectives

FOL uses the same major logical connectives as propositional logic.

AND
$$ \land $$

Example:

$$ Student(x) \land Intelligent(x) $$

Meaning:

x is a student AND x is intelligent.

OR
$$ \lor $$

Example:

$$ Student(x) \lor Teacher(x) $$

Meaning:

x is a student OR x is a teacher.

NOT
$$ \neg $$

Example:

$$ \neg Student(x) $$

Meaning:

x is not a student.

IMPLICATION
$$ \rightarrow $$

Example:

$$ Student(x) \rightarrow Person(x) $$

Meaning:

If x is a student, then x is a person.

BICONDITIONAL
$$ \leftrightarrow $$

Example:

$$ Student(x) \leftrightarrow Learner(x) $$

Meaning:

x is a student if and only if x is a learner.

9. Quantifiers

Quantifiers are one of the most important features of First-Order Logic.

There are two main quantifiers:

Universal Quantifier
Existential Quantifier
10. Universal Quantifier

The universal quantifier is:

$$ \forall $$

It means:

For all
For every

Example

All humans are mortal.

FOL representation:

$$ \forall x(Human(x) \rightarrow Mortal(x)) $$

Read as:

For every x, if x is a human, then x is mortal.

Another example

Every student studies.

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Studies(x)) $$
Important point

The universal quantifier does not mean that everything in the world is a student.

Consider:

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Intelligent(x)) $$

It means:

Every student is intelligent.

It does not mean:

Everyone is a student.

11. Existential Quantifier

The existential quantifier is:

$$ \exists $$

It means:

There exists at least one.

Example

Some student is intelligent.

$$ \exists x(Student(x) \land Intelligent(x)) $$

Read as:

There exists at least one x such that x is a student and x is intelligent.

Another example

There is a student who likes mathematics.

$$ \exists x(Student(x) \land Likes(x,Mathematics)) $$
12. Universal vs Existential Quantifier
Quantifier	Symbol	Meaning
Universal	\(\forall\)	For every
Existential	\(\exists\)	There exists at least one
Example

Every student is intelligent

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Intelligent(x)) $$

Some student is intelligent

$$ \exists x(Student(x) \land Intelligent(x)) $$

These two statements have different meanings.

13. Free and Bound Variables

A variable is bound when it is controlled by a quantifier.

Example:

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Intelligent(x)) $$

Here, x is bound by:

$$ \forall x $$

A variable that is not controlled by a quantifier is called a free variable.

Example:

$$ Student(x) $$

Here, x is free.

14. Well-Formed Formula

A valid expression in FOL is called a Well-Formed Formula (WFF).

Example:

$$ Student(Ram) $$

is a valid formula.

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Person(x)) $$

is also a valid formula.

15. Translating English into FOL

This is an important examination topic.

Example 1

All humans are intelligent.

$$ \forall x(Human(x) \rightarrow Intelligent(x)) $$
Example 2

Some humans are intelligent.

$$ \exists x(Human(x) \land Intelligent(x)) $$
Example 3

No human is immortal.

$$ \forall x(Human(x) \rightarrow \neg Immortal(x)) $$
Example 4

Ram is intelligent.

$$ Intelligent(Ram) $$
Example 5

Ram likes Sita.

$$ Likes(Ram,Sita) $$
Example 6

Every student likes mathematics.

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Likes(x,Mathematics)) $$
Example 7

There is a student who likes mathematics.

$$ \exists x(Student(x) \land Likes(x,Mathematics)) $$
16. Nested Quantifiers

FOL can use more than one quantifier in a statement.

Consider:

Every student likes some teacher.

We can represent it as:

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow \exists y(Teacher(y) \land Likes(x,y))) $$

Meaning:

For every student x, there exists some teacher y whom x likes.

Notice the order:

$$ \forall x\exists y $$

is different from:

$$ \exists y\forall x $$
17. Importance of Quantifier Order

Consider:

Statement 1

Every student likes some teacher.

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow \exists y(Teacher(y) \land Likes(x,y))) $$

Different students may like different teachers.

Statement 2

There is a teacher whom every student likes.

$$ \exists y(Teacher(y) \land \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Likes(x,y))) $$

Here, one particular teacher is liked by every student.

Therefore:

$$ \forall x\exists y $$

and

$$ \exists y\forall x $$

do not generally have the same meaning.

18. Examples of FOL Knowledge Representation

Suppose our domain contains students and teachers.

Facts

Ravi is a student.

$$ Student(Ravi) $$

Anu is a teacher.

$$ Teacher(Anu) $$

Ravi likes Anu.

$$ Likes(Ravi,Anu) $$
Rules

Every student is a person.

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Person(x)) $$

Every teacher is a person.

$$ \forall x(Teacher(x) \rightarrow Person(x)) $$

From:

$$ Student(Ravi) $$

and:

$$ \forall x(Student(x) \rightarrow Person(x)) $$

we can infer:

$$ Person(Ravi) $$
19. FOL Inference

Inference means deriving new knowledge from existing knowledge.

Consider:

Knowledge Base
$$ Human(Socrates) $$ $$ \forall x(Human(x) \rightarrow Mortal(x)) $$
Step 1

We know:

$$ Human(Socrates) $$
Step 2

The rule says:

$$ Human(x) \rightarrow Mortal(x) $$
Step 3

Substitute:

$$ x = Socrates $$

Therefore:

$$ Human(Socrates) \rightarrow Mortal(Socrates) $$
Conclusion
$$ Mortal(Socrates) $$

21. Advantages of First-Order Logic

FOL provides:

Greater expressive power
Representation of objects
Representation of properties
Representation of relationships
Representation of general rules
Quantification over objects
Formal reasoning and inference
Knowledge representation for AI systems
22. Limitations of FOL

FOL also has some limitations:

It can become complex for large knowledge bases.
Reasoning can be computationally expensive.
Basic FOL does not naturally represent uncertainty.
Representing time and changing information can be difficult.
Knowledge acquisition can be difficult.
Natural-language statements may have multiple interpretations.

For uncertainty, systems may use approaches such as Bayesian reasoning or Dempster-Shafer theory.

25. Simple Example for Students

Consider:

All engineers are educated.
Ravi is an engineer.
Therefore, Ravi is educated.

FOL representation

Rule:

$$ \forall x(Engineer(x) \rightarrow Educated(x)) $$

Fact:

$$ Engineer(Ravi) $$

Inference:

$$ Educated(Ravi) $$

This demonstrates how FOL represents knowledge and derives new knowledge through inference.

26. Summary

First-Order Logic extends propositional logic by introducing objects, variables, predicates, functions, and quantifiers.

The basic structure can be remembered as:

$$ \boxed{\text{Constants + Variables + Predicates + Functions + Connectives + Quantifiers}} $$

The two most important quantifiers are:

$$ \boxed{\forall = \text{For all}} $$ $$ \boxed{\exists = \text{There exists}} $$

The key idea is:

FOL allows AI systems to represent not only individual facts, but also general rules and relationships among objects.
