Representation of Knowledge – Knowledge Representation Issues
1. Introduction

Knowledge Representation (KR) is the process of representing real-world knowledge in a form that a computer system can store, understand, reason with, and use to make decisions.

In Artificial Intelligence, an intelligent system needs knowledge about:

Objects and their properties
Events and actions
Relationships between objects
Rules and constraints
Facts about the world
Causes and effects
Uncertainty and incomplete information
Example

Consider the statement:

“All humans are mortal. Socrates is a human. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.”

A knowledge representation system should store these facts and rules in such a way that it can infer the conclusion.

Human(Socrates)
∀x Human(x) → Mortal(x)
--------------------------------
Mortal(Socrates)

Thus, KR is not simply storing information. It also enables reasoning over the stored knowledge.

2. What is Knowledge Representation?
Definition

Knowledge Representation is the method of encoding knowledge about the world into a formal structure that can be processed and reasoned about by an AI system.

A good knowledge representation should allow an AI system to:

Represent facts.
Represent relationships.
Represent rules.
Draw new conclusions.
Handle changes in knowledge.
Handle incomplete or uncertain information.

5. Representational Adequacy
Meaning

Representational adequacy refers to the ability of a knowledge representation system to represent all the important types of knowledge required for a particular problem.

A KR system should be able to represent:

Objects
Properties
Relationships
Events
Actions
Rules
Concepts
Situations
Example

Suppose we want to represent:

“Ravi owns a car.”

We need to represent:

Person → Ravi
Object → Car
Relationship → Owns

A simple representation could be:

Owns(Ravi, Car1)

If we also want to represent:

“The car is red.”

we need:

Color(Car1, Red)
Key Point

A representation is representationally adequate if it can express the knowledge required by the application.

6. Inferential Adequacy
Meaning

Inferential adequacy is the ability of a knowledge representation system to derive new knowledge from existing knowledge.

The system should not only store facts but should also be able to reason with them.

Example

Given:

Human(Ravi)

and the rule:

Human(x) → Mortal(x)

The system should infer:

Mortal(Ravi)
Another Example
Bird(Tweety)
Bird(x) → HasWings(x)

Therefore:

HasWings(Tweety)
Key Point

Representational adequacy → Can I represent the knowledge?

Inferential adequacy → Can I derive new knowledge from it?

7. Inferential Efficiency
Meaning

Inferential efficiency refers to the ability of a knowledge representation system to perform reasoning efficiently.

An AI system may contain thousands or millions of facts and rules.

If it checks every fact for every query, reasoning can become very slow.

Example

Suppose a knowledge base contains:

1,000,000 facts
100,000 rules

If the system searches everything for every question, the response may be extremely slow.

Therefore, KR systems use techniques such as:

Indexing
Heuristics
Rule organization
Efficient search
Inference optimization
Example

If the question is:

“Can Ravi drive a car?”

The system should primarily search knowledge related to:

Ravi
Driver
Car
Driving

rather than examining unrelated information such as:

Weather on Mars
History of Rome
Population of Japan
Key Point

Inferential efficiency = obtaining conclusions with minimum unnecessary computation.

8. Acquisitional Efficiency
Meaning

Acquisitional efficiency refers to how easily new knowledge can be added to the knowledge base.

Knowledge is continuously changing.

For example:

Old:
Ravi works at Company A.

New:
Ravi works at Company B.

The system should make it easy to:

Add new facts
Modify existing facts
Delete outdated facts
Update rules
Example

A medical knowledge system may need regular updates when:

New diseases are discovered
New medicines are introduced
New medical guidelines are published
Key Point

A good KR system should make knowledge acquisition and updating easy and efficient.

9. Handling Incomplete Knowledge

Real-world knowledge is rarely complete.

An AI system may not know everything about an object.

Example

Suppose the system knows:

Bird(Tweety)

But it does not know:

Color(Tweety, ?)

The system should not automatically assume:

Color(Tweety, Red)

Instead, it should recognize that the information is unknown.

Three possible states

For a statement such as:

“Ravi owns a car.”

the system may know:

TRUE
FALSE
UNKNOWN

This is important because:

Unknown does not necessarily mean false.

10. Handling Uncertain Knowledge

Many real-world situations involve uncertainty.

Example

A doctor may say:

“The patient probably has influenza.”

This is not a definite fact.

Similarly:

“There is a 70% chance of rain tomorrow.”

An AI system needs mechanisms for representing uncertainty.

Common approaches include:

Probability
Bayesian networks
Fuzzy logic
Certainty factors
Possibility theory
Example
Rain tomorrow = 0.7

This represents uncertainty quantitatively.

11. Representing Common-Sense Knowledge

One of the biggest challenges in AI is representing common-sense knowledge.

Humans automatically understand many things that are rarely explicitly stated.

Example

Consider:

“John went to the restaurant and ordered food.”

Humans can infer that:

John is probably inside the restaurant.
Food is likely prepared before being served.
John may have eaten the food.
John probably paid or will pay for it.

However, these facts may not be explicitly stated.

Problem

There is an enormous amount of common-sense knowledge.

Examples:

Objects fall when unsupported.
People need food.
Fire can cause burns.
Water can make things wet.
People usually use doors to enter buildings.

Representing all such knowledge is difficult.

12. Representing Time

Knowledge often depends on time.

Consider:

“Ravi is a student.”

This may be true today but not necessarily five years later.

We may need to represent:

Student(Ravi, 2026)

and later:

Graduate(Ravi, 2029)
Temporal knowledge includes:
Before
After
During
Since
Until
Duration
Frequency
Example
Event A occurs before Event B.

Representation:

Before(A, B)
13. Representing Change

The world is dynamic.

Objects change their:

Location
State
Properties
Relationships
Example

Initially:

Door(Closed)

After an action:

Open(Door)

The KR system must represent:

Initial State → Action → New State

For example:

Closed(Door)
      ↓
OpenDoor()
      ↓
Open(Door)

This is important in:

Robotics
Game AI
Planning
Autonomous vehicles
14. Handling Consistency

A knowledge base may contain contradictory information.

Example

Suppose it contains:

Bird(Tweety)
CanFly(Tweety)

Later it receives:

CannotFly(Tweety)

Now there is a contradiction:

CanFly(Tweety)
CannotFly(Tweety)

A KR system needs mechanisms for:

Detecting contradictions
Resolving conflicts
Maintaining consistency
Determining which information is more reliable
15. Scalability

A knowledge representation system should work efficiently as the amount of knowledge increases.

Small knowledge base
100 facts

may be easy to process.

But a real-world AI system may contain:

Millions/Billions of facts

Therefore, the system must be able to:

Store large amounts of knowledge
Search efficiently
Perform inference efficiently
Update knowledge efficiently
16. Granularity of Knowledge

Another issue is deciding how detailed the representation should be.

Example

Suppose we represent:

“Ravi drove to college.”

A simple representation:

Drove(Ravi, College)

A detailed representation might include:

Driver = Ravi
Vehicle = Car1
StartingLocation = Home
Destination = College
Time = 8:00 AM
Speed = 40 km/h
Weather = Rainy
Problem

More detail provides more information but increases:

Storage requirements
Complexity
Reasoning time

Therefore, the appropriate level of granularity must be selected.

17. Choosing the Right Representation

Different types of knowledge may require different representation techniques.

Common knowledge representation techniques
Technique	Suitable for
Logic	Facts and formal reasoning
Production Rules	IF–THEN knowledge
Semantic Networks	Relationships between concepts
Frames	Structured object knowledge
Ontologies	Concepts, categories and relationships
Bayesian Networks	Uncertain knowledge
Fuzzy Logic	Vagueness
Scripts	Common sequences of events
Example

For:

“If it is raining, carry an umbrella.”

A production rule is appropriate:

IF Rain
THEN Carry(Umbrella)
18. Trade-off in Knowledge Representation

There is often a trade-off between:

Expressiveness
      ↕
Efficiency

A very expressive representation can represent complex knowledge, but reasoning may become computationally expensive.

A simple representation may be fast but unable to express complex knowledge.

Therefore:

A good KR system should provide a balance between expressiveness and computational efficiency.

19. Summary of Knowledge Representation Issues
Issue	Main Question
Representational Adequacy	Can the system represent required knowledge?
Inferential Adequacy	Can it derive new knowledge?

Acquisitional Efficiency	Can new knowledge be added easily?

Granularity	How much detail should be represented?
20. Important Exam Point

The four classical requirements of a knowledge representation system are:

1. Representational Adequacy

Ability to represent the required knowledge.

2. Inferential Adequacy

Ability to derive new knowledge from existing knowledge.

3. Inferential Efficiency

Ability to perform inference efficiently.

4. Acquisitional Efficiency

Ability to acquire and incorporate new knowledge efficiently.

Easy way to remember

R – I – I – A

Represent → Infer → Infer Efficiently → Acquire

Or:

“Represent knowledge, Infer new knowledge, Infer efficiently, Acquire knowledge easily.”

Knowledge and Its Use in Artificial Intelligence
1. What is Knowledge?

Knowledge is the collection of facts, information, concepts, relationships, rules, and experiences that an intelligent system can use to understand a situation, solve problems, and make decisions.

Simple definition

Knowledge is information about the world together with the relationships and rules needed to reason about that information.

For example, a human may know:

Birds have wings.
Birds generally can fly.
Penguins are birds.
Penguins cannot fly.

These are not just isolated facts. They are facts + relationships + rules, which can be used for reasoning.

2. Data, Information, and Knowledge

It is useful to distinguish these three concepts.

Data

Raw observations or values.

25
Ravi
80 km/h
Information

Data that has meaning.

Ravi's speed is 80 km/h.
Knowledge

Information combined with relationships, rules, and the ability to reason.

If a vehicle travels above the speed limit,
it may receive a speeding violation.

Ravi is travelling at 80 km/h.
Therefore, Ravi may be violating the speed limit.

So:

Data
  ↓
Information
  ↓
Knowledge
  ↓
Reasoning
  ↓
Decision
3. Types of Knowledge Used in AI

AI systems can work with different types of knowledge.

1. Factual Knowledge

Knowledge about facts.

Example:

The Earth revolves around the Sun.
Delhi is the capital of India.
2. Relational Knowledge

Knowledge about relationships between entities.

Example:

Ravi → student_of → ABC College
ABC College → located_in → Andhra Pradesh
3. Procedural Knowledge

Knowledge about how to perform an action.

Example:

To make tea:

Boil water
↓
Add tea powder
↓
Add milk
↓
Add sugar
↓
Serve
4. Heuristic Knowledge

Knowledge based on experience or rules of thumb.

Example:

If the road is wet, drive more carefully.

This is not an absolute law, but it can help an AI make a decision.

5. Common-Sense Knowledge

Everyday knowledge that humans normally assume.

Example:

If a glass falls on a hard floor, it may break.

6. Meta-Knowledge

Knowledge about knowledge.

Example:

“This rule is more reliable than that rule.”

An expert system may use this to decide which knowledge source to trust.

4. What is Knowledge in AI?

In AI, knowledge is information about a problem domain that is stored in a form that a computer can process and reason about.

The stored knowledge is generally called a:

Knowledge Base (KB)

For example, consider a medical AI.

Knowledge Base
│
├── Disease information
├── Symptoms
├── Causes
├── Medicines
├── Patient information
└── Diagnostic rules

The AI uses this knowledge to reason about a particular patient.

AI uses knowledge to solve problems.

Example

Suppose an AI has:

Fact:

Rule:

Fact:

The AI can reason:

Ravi is at Home
      +

      ↓

Thus, knowledge helps the AI find a solution.

7. Knowledge for Reasoning

AI can derive new knowledge from existing knowledge.

Example:

Fact:
Socrates is a human.

Rule:
All humans are mortal.

Inference:
Socrates is mortal.

In logical form:

Human(Socrates)

∀x Human(x) → Mortal(x)

Therefore:

Mortal(Socrates)

This process is called inference.

The computer cannot directly reason with knowledge in the same way humans express it.

Interact intelligently with humans

A useful way to remember this is:

18. Knowledge vs Intelligence

An important point for students:

Knowledge alone is not intelligence.

Intelligent behavior results from combining knowledge with perception, reasoning, learning, and action.
