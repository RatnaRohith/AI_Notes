Review of Probability

Probability is fundamental to reasoning under uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence. It provides a mathematical way to represent how likely an event is to occur when the available information is incomplete or uncertain.

1. Basic Terminology
Experiment

An action or process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty.

Example: Tossing a coin.

Sample Space \(S\)

The set of all possible outcomes.

For a coin:

$$ S=\{H,T\} $$

For a die:

$$ S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\} $$
Event

A subset of the sample space.

Example: Getting an even number when throwing a die:

$$ A=\{2,4,6\} $$
2. Probability of an Event

For equally likely outcomes:

$$ P(A)=\frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}} {\text{Total number of possible outcomes}} $$

Example:

Probability of getting a 4 on a die:

$$ P(4)=\frac{1}{6} $$

Probability of getting an even number:

$$ P(E)=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2} $$
3. Range of Probability

Probability always lies between 0 and 1:

$$ 0\leq P(A)\leq1 $$
Probability	Meaning
\(P(A)=0\)	Impossible event
\(0<P(A)<1\)	Uncertain event
\(P(A)=1\)	Certain event

Probability can also be expressed as a percentage.

$$ 0.75=75\% $$
4. Complement of an Event

The complement of event \(A\) means A does not occur.

It is represented by:

$$ \neg A $$

or

$$ A^c $$

The probability is:

$$ P(\neg A)=1-P(A) $$
Example

If the probability that it rains is:

$$ P(Rain)=0.3 $$

Then:

$$ P(No\ Rain)=1-0.3=0.7 $$
5. Addition Rule

The probability that A or B occurs is:

$$ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) $$

The intersection term is subtracted because it gets counted twice.

If A and B are mutually exclusive

If A and B cannot occur together:

$$ P(A\cap B)=0 $$

Therefore:

$$ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B) $$
Example

For a die:

$$ A=\{2\} $$ $$ B=\{4\} $$

Since they cannot occur simultaneously:

$$ P(A\cup B)=\frac16+\frac16=\frac13 $$
6. Conditional Probability

Conditional probability answers:

What is the probability of A occurring, given that B has already occurred?

It is written as:

$$ P(A|B) $$

and defined as:

$$ \boxed{P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}} $$

where \(P(B)>0\).

Example

Suppose:

60% of students study AI.
30% of students study AI and Machine Learning.

Then:

$$ P(ML|AI)=\frac{P(ML\cap AI)}{P(AI)} $$ $$ =\frac{0.30}{0.60}=0.5 $$

So there is a 50% probability that a student studies ML given that the student studies AI.

7. Independent Events

Two events are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

For independent events:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B) $$

Also:

$$ P(A|B)=P(A) $$
Example

Tossing a coin twice.

The result of the first toss does not affect the second toss.

Therefore:

$$ P(H_1\cap H_2)=P(H_1)P(H_2) $$ $$ =\frac12\times\frac12=\frac14 $$
8. Multiplication Rule

From conditional probability:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(A|B)P(B) $$

Similarly:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(B|A)P(A) $$

This rule is extremely important in AI because many AI problems involve the probability of multiple events occurring together.

9. Random Variables

A random variable assigns a numerical value to each possible outcome of a random experiment.

Example: Coin Toss

Let:

$$ X= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if Head}\\ 0 & \text{if Tail} \end{cases} $$

Here, \(X\) is a random variable.

Types

Discrete random variable

Takes countable values.

Examples:

Number of heads
Number of customers
Dice outcome

Continuous random variable

Can take any value within a range.

Examples:

Temperature
Height
Time
Speed
10. Probability Distribution

A probability distribution describes the probability associated with every possible value of a random variable.

For a fair die:

X	P(X)
1	1/6
2	1/6
3	1/6
4	1/6
5	1/6
6	1/6

The probabilities must add up to 1:

$$ \sum_xP(X=x)=1 $$
11. Joint Probability

Joint probability represents the probability that two or more events occur together.

$$ P(A,B) $$

is often used as shorthand for:

$$ P(A\cap B) $$

Example:

$$ P(Rain,Traffic) $$

means the probability that it is raining and there is heavy traffic.

This concept is especially important in Bayesian networks.

12. Marginal Probability

Marginal probability is the probability of one variable regardless of the value of another variable.

For example:

$$ P(Rain) $$

can be obtained from a joint distribution:

$$ P(Rain)=\sum_{t}P(Rain,t) $$

This process is called marginalization.

13. Bayes' Theorem

One of the most important concepts for AI reasoning under uncertainty is Bayes' theorem.

$$ \boxed{ P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)} } $$

It allows us to update our belief about \(A\) after obtaining evidence \(B\).

Components
$$ P(A) $$

= Prior probability

Initial belief about A.

$$ P(B|A) $$

= Likelihood

Probability of observing B if A is true.

$$ P(B) $$

= Evidence

Overall probability of observing B.

$$ P(A|B) $$

= Posterior probability

Updated belief about A after observing B.

AI Example

Suppose an AI medical diagnosis system wants to determine whether a patient has a disease based on a test result.

$$ P(Disease|Positive) $$

Bayes' theorem allows the system to calculate this from:

$$ P(Positive|Disease) $$ $$ P(Disease) $$

and

$$ P(Positive) $$

Thus, new evidence updates the AI's belief.

14. Expected Value

Expected value represents the average outcome expected over many repetitions.

For a discrete random variable:

$$ \boxed{ E(X)=\sum_x xP(X=x) } $$

Example:

For a fair die:

$$ E(X)= 1\left(\frac16\right)+ 2\left(\frac16\right)+ \cdots+ 6\left(\frac16\right) $$ $$ E(X)=3.5 $$

The expected value does not mean that we can roll a 3.5. It represents the long-run average.

15. Variance

Variance measures how much the values of a random variable differ from its expected value.

$$ Var(X)=E[(X-E(X))^2] $$

An equivalent formula is:

$$ Var(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2 $$

The standard deviation is:

$$ \sigma=\sqrt{Var(X)} $$
16. Probability Concepts Important in AI

The following concepts are particularly important for Reasoning Under Uncertainty:

Concept	Importance in AI
Probability	Represents uncertainty
Conditional Probability	Reasoning given evidence
Joint Probability	Represents relationships between events
Marginal Probability	Eliminates variables
Independence	Simplifies probability calculations
Bayes' Theorem	Updates beliefs using evidence
Random Variables	Represents uncertain quantities
Probability Distribution	Represents possible values and probabilities
Expected Value	Supports decision making
Variance	Measures uncertainty/spread
17. Probability in Artificial Intelligence

Traditional logic often works with:

True or False

Probability allows AI to work with:

How likely is it to be true?

For example:

Logic-based reasoning:

If smoke is detected, there is fire.

Probabilistic reasoning:

If smoke is detected, there is an 80% probability of fire.

This is useful because real-world AI systems deal with:

incomplete information
noisy sensors
uncertain observations
ambiguous situations
unreliable data
changing environments
Key Formulas to Remember
$$ \boxed{0\leq P(A)\leq1} $$ $$ \boxed{P(\neg A)=1-P(A)} $$ $$ \boxed{P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)} $$ $$ \boxed{P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}} $$ $$ \boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A|B)P(B)} $$

For independent events:

$$ \boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)} $$

Bayes' theorem:

$$ \boxed{P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}} $$

These provide the mathematical foundation for Bayesian reasoning, Bayesian networks, probabilistic inference, and decision-making under uncertainty in AI.
