Reasoning under uncertainty  
Reasoning Under Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence
1. Introduction

In many real-world AI problems, an agent does not have complete, accurate information about the environment.

For example:

A doctor may not know exactly which disease a patient has.
A self-driving car may not know whether an object is a pedestrian or a sign.
A weather system cannot know exactly whether it will rain tomorrow.
A robot may receive noisy sensor readings.
A spam filter cannot be 100% certain whether an email is spam.

Therefore, an intelligent system needs to reason with incomplete, uncertain, or ambiguous information.

Definition

Reasoning under uncertainty is the process of drawing conclusions or making decisions when the available knowledge is incomplete, ambiguous, noisy, or uncertain.

2. Why Uncertainty Occurs in AI

Uncertainty can arise for several reasons.

1. Incomplete Information

The system does not have all the required facts.

Example:

A medical AI knows:

Patient has fever.
Patient has cough.

But it does not know all the patient's medical history.

2. Noisy Information

Sensors or data sources may provide incorrect or imprecise information.

Example:

A robot's sensor reports:

"Obstacle detected at 4.9–5.1 meters."

The measurement is not exact.

3. Ambiguous Information

The same evidence may have multiple interpretations.

Example:

A camera detects an object.

It could be:

A person
A tree
A statue

The AI must determine the most likely interpretation.

4. Dynamic Environment

The world changes over time.

Example:

A traffic-navigation system cannot know exactly what traffic conditions will be 30 minutes from now.

5. Conflicting Information

Different sources may provide different information.

Example:

Sensor A:

Temperature = 30°C

Sensor B:

Temperature = 32°C

The AI must combine the evidence.

3. Certainty vs Uncertainty

Traditional logical reasoning often assumes that a statement is either:

True
False

For example:

All humans are mortal.
Socrates is a human.
Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

This is certain reasoning.

But real-world AI often deals with statements such as:

"There is a 70% probability that the patient has influenza."

This is uncertain reasoning.

Certain Reasoning	Uncertain Reasoning
True/False	Probability or degree of belief
Complete knowledge assumed	Incomplete knowledge allowed
Exact conclusions	Probabilistic conclusions
Classical logic	Probability, fuzzy logic, Bayesian reasoning
Example: Socrates is mortal	Example: Patient probably has flu

6. Probability-Based Reasoning

Probability provides a mathematical way to represent uncertainty.

Probability has values between:

$$ 0 \leq P(A) \leq 1 $$

where:

0 → impossible
1 → certain
values between 0 and 1 → different degrees of likelihood
Example

Suppose an AI predicts rain tomorrow:

$$ P(Rain)=0.8 $$

This means there is an 80% probability of rain.

It does not mean that rain is guaranteed.

7. Conditional Probability

Sometimes the probability of an event depends on another event.

This is called conditional probability.

$$ P(A|B) $$

It means:

Probability of A given that B has occurred.

Example

Let:

A = Patient has flu
B = Patient has fever

Then:

$$ P(Flu|Fever) $$

means:

Probability that the patient has flu given that the patient has fever.

8. Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem is one of the most important techniques for reasoning under uncertainty.

$$ P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)} $$

Where:

\(P(A|B)\) = posterior probability
\(P(B|A)\) = likelihood
\(P(A)\) = prior probability
\(P(B)\) = evidence
Basic idea
Prior Knowledge
      +
New Evidence
      ↓
Bayesian Reasoning
      ↓
Updated Belief
Example

A medical AI initially believes:

$$ P(Disease)=0.10 $$

After observing symptoms, it updates its belief:

$$ P(Disease|Symptoms)=0.65 $$

The system has updated its belief based on new evidence.

9. Bayesian Networks

A Bayesian Network is a graphical model used to represent probabilistic relationships among variables.

It consists of:

Nodes → variables
Directed edges → dependency relationships
Conditional probability tables → probabilities
Example
        Rain
       /    \
      ↓      ↓
   Wet Road  Traffic
      |
      ↓
   Accident

For example:

Rain increases probability of a wet road.
A wet road increases the probability of an accident.
Rain may also affect traffic.

Bayesian networks are useful for:

Medical diagnosis
Fault detection
Prediction
Risk analysis
Decision support
10. Fuzzy Logic

Probability and fuzzy logic are different concepts.

Probability

Deals with uncertainty about whether something is true.

Example:

There is a 70% probability that it will rain.

Fuzzy Logic

Deals with degrees of truth.

Example:

The temperature is somewhat hot.

A temperature of 30°C might have:

$$ Hot(30)=0.7 $$

This does not necessarily mean that there is a 70% chance that it is hot.

Instead, it represents the degree to which 30°C belongs to the concept "hot."

11. Certainty Factors

Certainty factors were used in expert systems such as MYCIN to represent confidence in conclusions.

For example:

IF patient has fever
AND patient has cough
THEN patient has flu
WITH certainty = 0.8

The system can therefore represent:

"The evidence supports this conclusion with a certain degree of confidence."

12. Dempster-Shafer Theory

Dempster-Shafer theory provides a framework for reasoning with belief and uncertainty.

Instead of assigning all belief to individual hypotheses, it can assign belief to a set of possibilities.

For example, an AI may know:

The object is either a car or a truck.

But it does not have enough evidence to decide which one.

Instead of forcing:

$$ P(Car)=0.5 $$

and

$$ P(Truck)=0.5 $$

the system can represent the uncertainty as belief in:

$$ \{Car, Truck\} $$

This is useful when evidence is incomplete.

13. Non-Monotonic Reasoning

In classical logic, once a conclusion is derived, adding new facts generally does not invalidate it.

Real-world reasoning is different.

Example

Initially:

Birds can fly.

We conclude:

Tweety can fly.

Then we learn:

Tweety is a penguin.

Now:

Tweety cannot normally fly.

The previous conclusion must be withdrawn.

This is called non-monotonic reasoning.

Initial Knowledge
       ↓
Conclusion
       ↓
New Information
       ↓
Revise Conclusion
14. Default Reasoning

Default reasoning allows an AI system to make reasonable assumptions when complete information is unavailable.

Example:

Birds normally fly.

If we know:

Tweety is a bird.

We assume:

Tweety can fly.

But if we later learn:

Tweety is a penguin.

The default assumption is cancelled.

15. Reasoning Under Uncertainty vs Reasoning with Incomplete Information

These concepts are related but not identical.

Incomplete information

The system does not know something.

Example:

"I don't know whether it will rain."

Uncertain information

The system has information but cannot be completely sure.

Example:

"There is an 80% chance of rain."

Concept	Example
Complete information	It will rain
Incomplete information	We don't know whether it will rain
Uncertain information	There is an 80% chance of rain
16. Example: Medical Diagnosis

Consider a medical diagnosis system.

Evidence
Patient
 ├── Fever
 ├── Cough
 ├── Fatigue
 └── Headache

Possible diseases:

             Symptoms
                 |
       +---------+---------+
       ↓         ↓         ↓
      Flu      COVID    Pneumonia

The AI may calculate:

Disease	Probability
Flu	0.60
COVID	0.25
Pneumonia	0.15

The system does not simply say:

"The patient definitely has flu."

Instead, it says:

"Based on the available evidence, flu is the most probable diagnosis."

This is reasoning under uncertainty.

17. Decision Making Under Uncertainty

Reasoning is often used to make decisions.

Suppose a self-driving car detects an object.

Possible interpretations:

Object
  |
  +-- Pedestrian → 70%
  |
  +-- Sign       → 20%
  |
  +-- Other      → 10%

The vehicle must decide whether to:

Continue
Slow down
Stop

Because the consequences of an incorrect decision may be serious, the AI considers both:

Probability + Cost of action

This leads to decision theory.

18. Expected Utility

A decision can be evaluated using expected utility.

$$ EU(Action)=\sum_i P(Result_i|Action)\times Utility(Result_i) $$

The AI chooses the action with the highest expected utility.

Example
              Decision
                 |
        +--------+--------+
        |                 |
      Brake            Continue
        |                 |
    Safe outcome     Possible accident

The system considers the probability and consequences of each outcome.

21. Advantages

Reasoning under uncertainty allows AI systems to:

Handle incomplete information
Deal with noisy data
Combine evidence from multiple sources
Make predictions
Estimate risks
Make decisions when certainty is impossible
Adapt conclusions when new information becomes available
22. Limitations

However, it also has challenges:

Probability values may be difficult to obtain.
Models can be computationally expensive.
Incorrect assumptions can produce incorrect conclusions.
Real-world dependencies may be difficult to model.
Different uncertainty models may produce different results.
Large Bayesian networks can become complex.
